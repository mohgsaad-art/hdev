## What's New in the 2023 Update
Since the report's first 2021 publication, the market developed rapidly: previously experience was almost entirely limited to converted/retrofitted vehicles, whereas by 2023 all major manufacturers have their own factory-built electric model. Battery and charging-infrastructure options have also matured. Update additions: a survey of **Brancheforeningen Cirkulær** (Danish industry association for the circular/waste sector) members, and a new section on grid-connected battery storage considerations for charging infrastructure. ## Introduction / Rationale for Electrifying Refuse Collection
- Refuse Collection Vehicles (RCV, Danish: renovationskøretøjer/skraldebiler) are considered particularly well suited to electrification, both operationally and from a user/community perspective: collection often happens in early morning hours in residential neighborhoods, so noise reduction is highly valued by both staff and residents.
- Operationally ideal: typically short, well-known routes with many stop-starts, and most of the route driven at low speed — though there is significant variation between urban and rural routes.
- The waste sector is simultaneously undergoing major change: Denmark's **"lov om sortering"** (sorting law), effective **1 July 2021**, has required municipalities to scale up rapidly to comply, particularly challenging given the increased number of waste fractions required in many municipalities; receiving facilities (modtageanlæg) are also being converted or closed, implying further change to delivery/drop-off points in coming years.

## Purpose and Main Conclusions
- The report aims to consolidate current experience and take stock of the broader market ("tage temperaturen på markedet") now and in the near future, to give municipalities and other stakeholders a decision-making basis for upcoming tenders or in-house takeover/outsourcing decisions.
- **Main conclusion:** the market is now mature ("markedet er modent nu"); major manufacturers are each introducing their own vision of the future refuse truck, viewing electricity as the obvious drivetrain choice for this segment.
- 2023 market is characterized both by new factory vehicles from major suppliers and continued development of vehicle conversions/retrofits (both new chassis and retrofitting used diesel vehicles).
- Despite better electric drivetrain operating economics and longer service life, electric RCVs remain **substantially more expensive to purchase** than equivalent diesel vehicles — extra budget allocation will be needed for the transition for some time yet, both for vehicle purchase and for charging infrastructure.
- Positive experience is reported from municipalities and carriers that have taken the first steps. The first edition of the report highlighted **Frederiksberg Kommune** (8 years of operation at that time) with no more operational difficulties than diesel vehicles — though Frederiksberg benefits from flat terrain and a small area (shorter routes). More hilly terrain or longer rural routes can challenge electric RCV range or require other drivetrains — addressed via a new case from **Vejle Kommune**, one of Denmark's most hilly areas, where **Urbaser** has now deployed electric RCVs.
- Positive experience extends to climate/environment and to working environment/community reception: strong "signal value" to citizens, and reduced noise/pollution is noticed and appreciated.

## Section: Initial Considerations for Electrifying Refuse Collection

### Political Context
The market is still at a stage where cost is generally weighed against environment/climate; political backing for an electrification strategy is required given the substantial cost involved. In many municipalities this political backing appears present and is often the actual driver of electrification.

### Routes
Initial assessment should focus on evaluating current routes: is electrification technically/economically relevant, and should focus initially be on only part of the routes? General advantage of refuse collection: known daily routes, typically not long — **often around 50–150 km**. Collection driving itself is at relatively low speed (especially in cities) with many stop-starts, optimal for regenerative braking with correct driving technique. Key route parameters: route length, terrain (hilly/flat), distance to disposal/tipping site, and whether multiple daily emptyings are needed. Analysis may show some routes are more suited to electrification than others — a phased start with a subset of routes has additional benefits for staff familiarization, charging infrastructure buildout, and fallback safety in case of breakdown.

### Range / Capacity
A key parameter is vehicle range, determined by battery capacity. **Important caution:** buyers should be aware of the difference between the manufacturer-stated **nominal capacity** and the **usable capacity** of the battery — these can differ substantially. This is attributed to manufacturers reserving a buffer at the top and bottom of the battery's charge range (not used) to reduce degradation and meet durability/warranty requirements at a level far stricter than for passenger cars — meaning actual usable range can be less than the nominal battery size would suggest.

### Charging Infrastructure
A key and essential challenge distinct from private EV charging: establishing charging facilities for an entire fleet requires potentially large energy quantities and the ability to manage/control charging. Since Denmark has a **universal supply obligation (forsyningspligt)**, in principle the challenge reduces to time and cost — what it costs to bring X amperes to the depot and to install/operate the necessary chargers, and how long connection takes. For larger installations, serious considerations arise around charging windows, route planning, battery size, vehicle charging power, and — critically — **who is responsible for establishing infrastructure if collection is outsourced**. Should charging be arranged by the municipality, shared with other companies/users, or included in the tender? The answer depends heavily on the municipality's long-term waste-collection strategy. A key consideration flagged: this type of investment is relatively long-term (**10+ years for charging equipment, 30+ years for electrical infrastructure**) — is it well suited to being folded into tender periods that might only run 6–8 years?

### Economics
Climate and environment come at a cost, particularly in an early-stage market with limited competition and limited operating experience. If the municipality self-operates collection, a large initial cost for equipment purchase and charging infrastructure should be expected, plus extra resources for staff onboarding; some of the extra initial cost can be expected to be recouped via lower fuel, service, and maintenance costs during operation. If collection is outsourced, investment/risk sits with the carrier/haulier, who in a new low-margin market must price in a risk buffer when bidding.
- Large initial costs could affect contract period length: amortizing an RCV and infrastructure over 10 years instead of the current typical norm of 6–8 years would lower annual costs, generally in everyone's interest — but this is complicated by ongoing regulatory change in waste sorting/fraction requirements, which is hard to build into fixed long-term contracts.
- Because charging infrastructure has a markedly longer amortization horizon than the vehicles, the report suggests considering **separate tenders for vehicles vs. charging infrastructure** — infrastructure amortized perhaps over 30 years (electrical portion) vs. vehicles/chargers over e.g. 10 years — balancing reasonable economics for the municipality against reasonable risk coverage for the waste company.

### Climate, Environment and Working Environment
The clear "winners" from electrification: climate, environment, and working environment. Climate benefit is large relative to diesel and grows yearly as the energy mix becomes greener and systematic battery recycling scales up. Environmentally: all local particulate/gas emissions eliminated; noise emissions also reduced, benefiting both the urban near-environment and the working environment of refuse workers (who also avoid vibration from a running diesel engine). References the "**triple bottom line**" framework (Economy, People, Environment) used by increasingly many companies/public bodies to frame priorities — the report does not itself weigh these factors, leaving that to individual municipalities/companies. Footnote: further reading on real environmental benefits of switching to electric trucks available at **elbilviden.dk**, which hosts an **LCA (Life Cycle Analysis)** tool for comparing drivetrains.

## Experience with Operating Electric Refuse Trucks

### History
Major manufacturers (Scania, Volvo, Mercedes etc.) have only recently begun series production, but electric truck/RCV development dates back **more than 25 years**. Early vehicles used lead-acid batteries; approximately **12 years ago** (i.e., ~2011) the first lithium-battery RCVs emerged, accelerating development. French **PVI** was a pioneer with converted electric RCVs, one of the first to introduce lithium-battery electric drivetrain RCVs; Dutch **Emoss** later also entered the market.

## Danish Experience — Case Studies

### Frederiksberg Kommune
| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Citizens | 104,000 |
| Waste collection taken in-house (hjemtaget) | 2012 |
| Electric RCV operating experience since | 2013 |
| Total RCVs | 25 |
| Electric RCVs | 21 |
| Planned further purchases | None currently planned |

- First Danish municipality to deploy an electric RCV (2013). Collection was taken in-house in 2010; **Frederiksberg Gartner og Vej (FGV)** already had experience with electric vehicles (cargo bikes, tool carriers) and believed electric should be the preferred choice for waste collection too, for multiple reasons.
- Vehicles from **PVI**, and from 2023 also **Volvo**.
- **Tender/market dialogue:** In 2012, only one manufacturer — French **PVI (Power Vehicle Innovation)**, via Danish partner **Phoenix Danmark** — could meet the municipality's requirement for operational experience with electric RCVs on the Danish market at that time.
- **Vehicle:** built on a conventional **Dennis Eagle chassis**, with which the municipality had good prior experience. Concerns at the time: operational reliability, load capacity, how batteries would perform in Danish climate, and the fact the vehicle was a conversion rather than purpose-built from the start.
- **Charging infrastructure:** PVI vehicles charge via a **CEE plug drawing up to 63 amperes**. The municipality invested **2.5 million DKK across two expansion phases** of power capacity: first going from 1 to 5 vehicles, then from 5 to 20 vehicles — no major challenges except a relatively long delivery time, currently around **8–10 months** in the Copenhagen area.
- Space is limited at the depot, so vehicle placement planning matters — electric RCVs have fixed charging spots and take up more space, and are far less mobile/flexible than conventional vehicles, so securing a durable location is important given the cost of later restructuring.
- **Operational reliability:** positive surprise. Frederiksberg deliberately chose a controlled phase-in, starting with a single vehicle and gradually expanding. Early teething issues included gear-shifting uphill and a lift mechanism that was initially awkward to operate. Though the RCV entered service after a month, it took **6 months** to become fully "run in." Cooperation with supplier **Phoenix** (which supplies all the vehicles) was described as very good; over time, electric RCVs in Frederiksberg Kommune achieved **as many operating hours as the conventional diesel vehicles**.
- **Batteries/capacity/load:** real concern about cold-climate battery performance did not materialize — unlike lead-acid batteries (which lost capacity sharply in cold weather in the municipality's prior vehicle experience), lithium-ion batteries performed fine; cabin heating works well via a small oil-fired heater. Phoenix Danmark has a vehicle operating in Norway that has reached temperatures as low as **-20°C**, requiring heating elements around the battery pack, whereas vehicles in Paris and Lyon instead have battery cooling — but neither measure has proven necessary in the Danish climate to date.
- The **PVI C LESS 26** model has **2 tonnes less load capacity** than the other diesel vehicles used in Frederiksberg, combined with a limited range of **120–150 km**, initially raising concern — but this has not required route changes in practice. The municipality has since moved fully to the electric RCV variant with the larger **255 kWh battery**, for flexibility ahead of possible future district reorganization; newer vehicles are also rated up to **27 tonnes**.
- **Since the 2021 first report**, Frederiksberg has added **10 further electric vehicles**, now from **Volvo**, across 3 different models:
  - **4x Volvo FL** box vans (bulky-waste collection vehicles)
  - **5x Volvo FE** refuse collection vehicles (low-entry cab for household waste)
  - **1x Volvo FM 8x4** with crane and hook lift
- As of **May 2023**, Frederiksberg Kommune's **21 electric refuse vehicles** constitute the **second-largest such fleet in the Nordics**, surpassed only by **ARC**.
- Approach described as "laying the tracks while the train is running" — lesson learned: planning is not a major issue with one or two electric vehicles, but scaling to e.g. 20 is where planning becomes critical, both for charging infrastructure and for assessing current/future driving needs relative to range and operating time.

**Range (Aktionsradius) vs. Operating Time (Aktionstid):**
- Two distinct concepts distinguished: **Aktionsradius** (range) = km an electric RCV can drive on one charge (can be calculated with or without in-body compaction); **Aktionstid** (operating time) = time during which an RCV can drive and collect waste on one charge, factoring in number of emptyings and driving patterns.
- Frederiksberg has dense urban driving with few km but many stops. Longest operating day: **05:45–17:30**.
- On Frederiksberg, daily distance rarely exceeds **70 km** under two-shift operation, but even so the smaller **175 kWh** battery drops to **15%** charge — deemed operationally unsuitable, so the municipality now runs the larger **255 kWh** battery exclusively. Clear conclusion stated: it is just as important to discuss operating time as range.
- Overall daily working time for the RCV: **8–9 hours**, given high parasitic power draw from the compactor and automatic lift, plus distance to the tipping/disposal site.

**Staff Onboarding:**
- Another reason for the controlled phase-in: driver/staff adaptation takes time and varies by individual; once the first three vehicles are operational, subsequent onboarding becomes easier as knowledge/experience spreads among staff.
- Quote (Thomas Madsen, Driftsleder/Operations Manager): *"De første tre nye biler af en type er de sværeste. Ved den 4. bil så kender medarbejderne fordele og ulemper og de indgår meget let i driften"* ("The first three new vehicles of a type are the hardest. By the 4th vehicle, staff know the pros and cons and they integrate very easily into operations.")
- Takes up to **6 months** to select and train the right staff to a level where they can operate the vehicle well and become ambassadors for electric operation. Thorough training in close cooperation with the supplier is recommended, ideally with a feedback mechanism.
- Even carefully selected drivers initially struggled to see the benefits, focused on slower acceleration/top speed and gear-control issues. Quote: *"Vi havde bl.a. problemer med gearskiftet op ad Valby bakke, hvilket blev klaret med en ny gearfunktion, som nu er standard i de nye biler, der bliver leveret"* ("We had problems with gear shifting up Valby hill, which was solved with a new gear function that is now standard in the new vehicles being delivered.") — Thomas Madsen.
- Despite the feedback mechanism and majority staff buy-in, **after 7 years with electric RCVs, a few staff members still have not come around**.

### Vejle Kommune
| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Citizens | ~118,000 |
| Waste collection outsourced to | Urbaser |
| Electric RCV experience since | April 2023 |
| Total RCVs | 23 |

- Vejle Kommune covers **1,066 km²**, with **~58,000 households** and **~300 municipal institutions**; ~118,000 residents, now Denmark's **5th-largest municipality** by population, expected to grow ~1%/year.
- Vejle is highly ambitious on climate and required electric and gas vehicles in its waste-collection tender.
- **Urbaser A/S** won the contract, bidding **5 pure electric eEconic RCVs** and **18 gas-powered RCVs** to cover all waste fractions, as required by the tender.
- Electric eEconic deployment: **3 electric trucks** for residual/bio waste collection; **2 electric trucks** for recyclable waste collection.
- Vejle has very hilly terrain (e.g. **Bredballe**, described as having many steep hills), causing higher kWh consumption climbing — but also good opportunity for battery regeneration via motor/brake effect on downhill sections, energy that would be wasted with diesel vehicles.
- eEconic vehicles return from a working day with **28–43% battery remaining**; drivers reportedly very positive about driving them.
- kWh/km consumption is at a "reasonable average" but varies somewhat between individual vehicles — **Urbaser A/S expects this can be evened out** through driver training and eco-driving technique coaching.

**ARC's experience introducing electric RCVs:**
- As part of the plan to make Copenhagen the world's **first CO2-neutral capital by 2025**, Copenhagen City Hall politicians decided in **2018** to electrify waste collection — a bold decision at the time, described as "going all-in." Prior to this, Copenhagen's RCVs already ran on certified biogas with electric lift/compaction, so electrification of the drivetrain was the final step in the green transition.
- Household waste collection across Copenhagen's **10 city districts** was, at that time, outsourced to several private carriers under district-level contracts. The political decision to electrify was bundled with a decision to **bring collection in-house (hjemtagning)**, to be phased in as each district's carrier contract expired, with **ARC** taking over future collection.
- Bundling electrification with in-house takeover made sense on some levels but was challenging on others for ARC — expecting more optimization/flexibility potential once operating the whole city at once rather than district by district. A major challenge: no consolidated historical driving/route data existed across all districts to simulate the future citywide scenario for planning.
- As of **April 2023**, ARC operates **2 districts**, with the **last 8 districts** taken over from **1 May 2023**. In practice, this meant conducting **EU tenders** for the entire electric RCV fleet before even a single vehicle had been delivered — described as "a bit challenging."
- Quote: *"Vi gennemførte alle EU-udbud af køretøjerne inden vi fik det første køretøj leveret – en anelse udfordrende!"* (Claus Barslund, Driftschef/Operations Director, ARC)

**Vehicles:**
- Initial tender ambitions were higher, but given the immature market, requirements were adjusted to match what was actually available while still ensuring competitive bidding — target of at least **2 suppliers** able to bid, to ensure some competition.
- Working-environment representatives were consulted in specifying vehicle requirements — down to details like glove-box cooling and a **glove dryer**, which has proven to have real value for staff (previously gloves were inconveniently placed on top of the heater; now dried via a small dedicated dryer, "mest af alt minder om en lille brødrister" — "most resembles a small toaster").
- Vehicles well received by refuse workers; ARC reports "glade og positive medarbejdere" (happy and positive employees), including examples of staff who had been driving gas vehicles and, after borrowing an electric vehicle during servicing for a few days, specifically requested to move to electric permanently — attributed significantly to the markedly reduced noise level.
- Quote: *"Vi har eksempler på medarbejdere der var vant til at køre på gas og som i forbindelse med service lånte en el-bil efterfølgende bad om at kommer over på el-bilen permanent"* (Driftschef ARC / Operations Director, ARC).
- First electric vehicle entered service **January 2022**; vehicles have "largely positively surprised." Expected "teething issues" ("børnesygdomme") with first-generation new-technology vehicles have occurred; ARC works closely with manufacturers to resolve them.
- ARC has **109 electric vehicles** total, majority from **SCANIA**, of which **68 are 3-axle compactor vehicles**; also a number of **2-axle vehicles**, advantageous for maneuverability and space, particularly in inner Copenhagen where accessibility is challenging. Distance/range has not been an issue in practice; routes have been planned without significant battery-related challenges. Operations are organized around available vehicles/battery sizes — number of bins per route matters, and seasonal factors like low temperatures can slightly reduce electric RCV range. The 2-axle vehicles, easier to maneuver in the inner city, handle lighter fractions well (e.g., cardboard and plastic).
- Supplier-provided driver training under the contracts: as of **April 2023**, **120 staff** had completed training, with a further **250 to follow**. Training is a 2–3 hour introductory course with ongoing follow-up; over time, more peer-to-peer ("sidemandsoplæring") training is expected as more vehicles enter service. Vehicles are now delivered well ahead of entering service, allowing training on non-operational vehicles — seen as a major advantage.

**First-mover challenges:**
- ARC has been a first-mover for non-converted (factory) vehicles from major producers, combined with rapid fleet scale-up and suppliers themselves using new sub-suppliers on first deliveries — has clearly caused challenges throughout the supply chain, not usually with individual components but more often with **integration** issues, especially early on. Suppliers have generally "manned up" and worked with ARC to resolve issues as they arise. Operations run 24/7 and waste must be collected regardless.

**Charging infrastructure:**
- ARC specified use of **CCS Combo 2 plugs** with **25 kW charging power**. At full planned fleet scale (expected 130 vehicles), this implied a power supply requirement of **3,500–4,000 amperes per site** — a very substantial power demand, acknowledged in dialogue with grid operator **Radius**. Where other charging-infrastructure projects might request a few hundred amperes, a connection at this scale required multi-party planning, prompting renewed reconsideration and underscoring ARC's role as a first-mover in large-scale charging infrastructure.
- The grid operator could not deliver supply at the originally desired scale, so in practice ARC established an **additional site** for some vehicles and had to **downgrade planned charger power**, ending up buying chargers with less flexibility in charging windows (charging between **21:00–06:00** and between first and second shift). Original intent was **50 kW DC chargers**, but due to insufficient available power, ARC instead installed primarily **24 kW DC chargers**, supplemented by **100 kW chargers** to cover the need to recharge between the first and second shifts.
- Beyond supply-delivery challenges, ARC has also experienced **teething problems in charger operation** — as of writing (April 2023), continuing stability issues with charging sessions, e.g., chargers stopping mid-session, requiring ARC staff to physically restart charging. Suppliers suspect a communication/interpretation issue between vehicle and charger; diagnosis and resolution were in progress.
- **DC charging** was a requirement since market signals indicated this as the direction of travel; DC also allows higher charging power (e.g. 100 kW), important for ARC's need to recharge between first and second shifts.

**Fire and Emergency Preparedness:**
- ARC's program management placed strong focus on fire/emergency planning specific to large-scale electrical and battery installations. Initially difficult to obtain concrete, precise information on this, but through ARC's work and broader sector development, emergency services have since built up knowledge and standards across municipalities. (Footnote references **elbilviden.dk** — https://elbilviden.dk/privat/teknisk-viden-om/brand-i-elbiler/ — for further technical information on EV fires.)

- Another capital-region municipality choosing to switch a larger fleet share to electric, inspired by Frederiksberg Kommune's good experience; invested in a larger electric fleet in connection with bringing collection in-house.
- Originally budgeted/planned for **9 electric RCVs**, but vehicle pricing led to an initial purchase of **7 electric RCVs** (what fit the budget); plan to acquire a further **4–5 vehicles**, reaching **12 electric RCVs** out of the total fleet of **16**.
- **2023 update:** a recent tender resulted in an order for **3 further electric RCVs**, this time **Mercedes eEconic**.
- Vehicles acquired following a political agreement to bring waste collection in-house; based on close contact with Frederiksberg Kommune, chose **PVI vehicles delivered by Phoenix Danmark**. Rødovre found it difficult to build sufficient flexibility into subcontractor contracts and consequently took collection in-house, partly as a consequence of increasing waste-sorting requirements and ongoing sector changes.
- Vehicles are primarily **2-chamber with automatic lift**; the automatic lift, which eases operation, has been very well received by refuse workers, who see it as the biggest advantage of the new electric RCVs.
- After about a year in operation: **no more faults** on the electric vehicles compared to equivalent diesel vehicles. The **255 kWh** battery comfortably handles the route, usually with **over 50%** remaining on return.
- Noted limitation: fully loaded, vehicles can only reach about **35 km/h on even a slight incline** — causing some difficulty returning to **Vestforbrændingen** (waste treatment facility), where they must merge into an overtaking lane to enter the site. An area the municipality would like improved, though it doesn't change the overall positive experience for refuse workers, who "definitely don't miss the old diesel choppers."
- Quote (Mathias Pettersson, Skraldemand/Refuse Worker): *"Vi måtte på et tidspunkt tilbage i vores gamle vogn for en kort periode, da vores el-skraldebil var på værksted. Der fik vi nærmest et chok over, hvor stor forskellen var med hensyn til støj og det manuelle arbejde."* ("At one point we had to go back to our old vehicle for a short period while our electric RCV was in the workshop. We were almost shocked at how big the difference was in terms of noise and manual work.")

One of Denmark's most climate-ambitious municipalities via the **"Project Zero"** initiative, focused heavily on CO2 reduction. ## Market Survey — Transition to Electric RCVs (Spring 2023)
Conducted in spring 2023 among members of **Brancheforeningen Cirkulær** (Danish trade association for the circular economy/waste sector), in collaboration with that association, to gauge municipal sentiment on electrifying waste collection. Four central survey questions highlighted:

1. **"Is there support to allocate the necessary extra funds for the transition to low-emission vehicles?"** — Large majority of respondents reported political backing; only **3 respondents (8%)** answered "to a limited extent," and **27%** answered "to some extent." Interpreted positively — most municipalities appear ready to find the extra budget needed.

2. **"Has the municipality analyzed collection routes with a view to optimization for the transition to low-emission vehicles?"** — Only a small share of municipalities had examined route optimization in depth. Likely explained by the fact many municipalities have outsourced collection and thus do not engage in the more operational planning, instead only setting emission requirements in tenders. This may mean municipalities lack full insight into optimization/emission-reduction opportunities and the economic possibilities newer driving patterns could offer. Feedback in several cases indicated municipalities need good, updated, correct guidance to ensure a proper analytical basis for decisions, especially in a fast-moving market.

3. **"Have you considered charging infrastructure for refuse trucks?"** — **71%** of respondents said they had **not** considered this — flagged by the report as a substantial missed opportunity for municipalities to engage actively, particularly where collection is outsourced. The report stresses this is a central area to examine both for needs elsewhere in the municipal fleet and for aligning tender conditions with a market largely transitioning to electric, given charging infrastructure's different (longer) investment/amortization horizon.

4. **"What are the electrification plans for the next 5 years — what share should be electrified in the next tender round?"** — Highly varied answers, from 0% to 100% electrified, but with a **majority around 30–50%**. Hard to answer precisely partly because many municipalities have not analyzed the area closely and instead work on a "business as usual" basis where collection is outsourced and carriers make most electrification decisions. Report also notes that until recently it has been genuinely difficult to source electric RCVs at all — much of the current fleet is only about a year old and is "version 1" technology — municipalities may understandably have wanted to wait and see how vehicles perform in practice, a stage the market has now largely moved past, per the report.

## Charging Infrastructure — Detailed Section

### Economic-Tactical Considerations
Two basic scenarios: (1) municipality self-operates collection — must both acquire electric vehicles and build necessary charging infrastructure, the latter as a long-term investment amortized over years; (2) collection is outsourced — general pattern is that infrastructure investment falls to the carrier, mirroring today's situation where the municipality doesn't concern itself with how/where the carrier fuels up with diesel — that responsibility sits with the carrier. Where the municipality does not actively engage in dialogue with the carrier on this topic, responsibility and risk sit with the carrier, who must then price in the risk of an infrastructure investment that must either be amortized over a short period or gambled on contract renewal.

### Contracts with Carriers
Vehicle price alone already challenges contracts — electric RCVs typically cost **double** equivalent diesel vehicles, and the shorter the amortization period, the more this burdens total economics. Charging infrastructure skews the picture further: infrastructure investments can have a lifespan of **30–40 years**, whereas waste-collection contract periods are typically **5–6 years** (occasionally with up to a 2-year extension option). If a carrier's contract is not renewed, the carrier may be left with a large charging-infrastructure investment of little or no remaining value; equally, a new contract winner must factor in the cost of building costly new charging infrastructure themselves. The report suggests municipalities should actively consider how to address this cost issue — options include: municipality itself providing infrastructure; carrier providing it; shared infrastructure with other relevant users (buses, taxis, other municipal vehicles, etc.); and infrastructure placement decisions relative to the above and to potential future carrier changes.

### Charging Solution — Bus Depots in Esbjerg and Vejle (Inspirational Example)
**Esbjerg Kommune** built a bus depot as part of its city bus tender, with the depot provided free of charge to whichever operator won the electric city bus contract; buses entered service **December 2021**. This decouples the depot/charging infrastructure (pre-built by the municipality) from the operating service itself. Bus contract period: **10.5 years**; bus depot amortized over **24 years**.

**Vejle** followed a similar approach: the bus depot offers a range of driver/maintenance services for an annual rental fee, plus the necessary **2,200 ampere** grid connection included in the rental agreement, covering **29 electric buses**; the winning operator can purchase additional supply capacity at their own cost if needed. The report suggests this model — decoupling long-term infrastructure investment from shorter-term operator selection — has clear advantages for both parties and is interesting to consider for the refuse-collection sector too, potentially allowing a winning depot operator to offer charging to other vehicle types (distribution vans, taxis, private cars, or refuse trucks) during daytime hours when buses are out on routes.

### Charging Infrastructure — AC or DC Charging
Key distinction: whether vehicles have onboard chargers or not. If not (as several major producers have chosen), charging must occur via external DC charging stations.

**AC charging:** requires an onboard charger. For truck-segment large batteries, relatively high-power chargers are needed for overnight charging (depending on the charging window available). E.g., a **275 kWh** battery charged over 10 hours needs **27.5 kW** delivered (plus ~3–4% charging loss) to go from 0–100%. This exceeds standard **22 kW Type 2** charge boxes if starting from fully depleted (unlikely in practice). The Type 2 standard supports up to **43 kW**, so relatively powerful AC chargers via Type 2 are possible, but few RCVs currently support charging above 22 kW. An alternative is a powerful onboard charger with a **red CEE plug** — PVI has chosen this route, achieving **45 kW** charging with a 63A supply. Main advantage of onboard chargers: simpler infrastructure — essentially just a CEE plug the vehicle connects to. Despite the Type 2 standard's higher-power potential, only a few RCVs support it; **Volvo/Renault** (besides PVI, the only other AC-charging offerors) support a maximum **22 kW** AC charging power.

**DC charging:** several major manufacturers pursue a DC-only strategy, requiring the charger, inverter, and power electronics to be sited externally rather than on the vehicle. DC equipment can be quite costly: **50–75 kW** DC charging stations typically cost between **100,000 and 200,000 DKK**, depending on quality/functionality; adding service agreements, connection, and installation, a single DC station's total cost can easily reach **300,000 DKK**. Once installed, a DC charger is, by nature, a stationary asset. Truck manufacturers have largely partnered with charging equipment suppliers rather than developing this themselves — buyers are not obligated to use the partner supplier, though it is sometimes recommended; the report notes no evidence of manufacturers insisting exclusively on their named partner.

### AC and DC Charging Infrastructure — Price Examples
Three illustrative cost scenarios (excluding excavation/electrical groundwork, which is highly location-specific; all prices exclude VAT):

**Scenario 1 — AC charging via CEE plug (grid connection only):**
- If no existing supply: approx. **16,400 DKK** for the first 25A, then approx. **1,200 DKK per additional A** up to the nearest standard fuse size (e.g. 35, 50, 63, 80, 100, 160A).
- Connecting at 35A or 63A gives **22 kW** or **43.5 kW** respectively.
- Total example price: **28,400 DKK** (at 35A) or **62,000 DKK** (at 63A).

**Scenario 2 — AC charging via Type 2 plug:**
- As above, plus a charge box (possibly with networking/load balancing).
- A 22 kW professional-use charge box costs approx. **12,500 DKK** per unit.
- Total example price: **40,900 DKK** (35A) or **74,500 DKK** (63A) connection including the charge box.

**Scenario 3 — DC charging:**
- Power needs must be carefully assessed: a 50 kW station requires an **80A** connection for full power; a 75 kW station requires **125A**. Station cost: approx. **120,000–175,000 DKK** for 50 kW; approx. **200,000 DKK** for 75 kW (varying with quality/functionality). Add installation/contracting cost of **50,000–100,000 DKK**.
- Example total investment for one 50 kW station:
  - Connection (80A): **82,400 DKK**
  - Charging station (50 kW): **150,000 DKK**
  - Installation and contracting: **75,000 DKK**
  - **Total: 307,400 DKK**
- Note: small **25 kW** DC chargers can be purchased for **under 100,000 DKK**.
- Potential savings from multiple vehicles sharing one multi-outlet station with load balancing (power per outlet adjusted to total combined load), though this depends heavily on actual power needs, station capacity calculations, available supply, and physical site conditions.

### Opportunity Charging at Disposal/Drop-Off Sites
Strongly recommended consideration: installing DC chargers at waste disposal/drop-off sites, which can serve as a natural mid-route charging stop if needed, avoiding a detour back to the depot. Relevant given battery capacity naturally degrades over time, or in very cold weather affecting efficiency and range, or during periods of extra collection need (Christmas, holidays, etc.) where an extra trip home to charge — or worse, running out of charge before returning — is undesirable. Tactically/economically, having this opportunity-charging option available allows purchasing vehicles with **smaller batteries** or planning **longer routes**, reducing vehicle purchase price and overall TCO by allowing lower battery safety margins.

### Charging Infrastructure and the Electricity Grid
Denmark has a **universal supply obligation (forsyningspligt)**: grid companies are obliged to supply power to consumers, private or commercial, including for charging infrastructure — but in the current high-demand market, **delivery/connection time** is often the limiting factor rather than obligation itself.

For smaller-scale charging infrastructure (a few hundred amperes via the **0.4 kV network**), pricing is simple: pay only for the number of amperes needed, with all operational/service responsibility sitting with the grid company. At larger scale, the customer becomes responsible for parts of the network themselves, causing total prices to vary significantly depending on connection type agreed with the grid company. Connection type depends on the specific installation's needs plus many local factors (existing high-voltage network installation, customer type, service agreements available for equipment the customer is responsible for).

**Connection categories** (per grid company **Radius**, source: Radius, 2021): customers are placed in a category — **C, B-lav (B-low), B-høj (B-high), A-lav (A-low)**, etc. — based on power needs. Per-ampere connection prices don't vary hugely between categories (slightly cheaper per ampere at higher categories), but **ownership of, and responsibility for operating, the equipment** differs significantly and drives most of the final price difference. Key threshold points (per Radius table, 2021) occur at **750 amperes** and **4,500 amperes**, where responsibility for parts of the network shifts to the customer.

**Radius** (responsible for the electricity grid in Copenhagen, North Zealand, and parts of West Zealand) continually expands the grid as needs arise, and recommends engaging in dialogue **early** in a project's process to secure clarity on costs and delivery timelines. From Radius's perspective, early engagement allows better planning/prioritization of grid expansion in given areas and faster delivery of the requested capacity. Radius explicitly encourages municipalities to develop a dedicated **charging-station strategy (ladestanderstrategi)** covering all relevant areas of EV charging need. Quote (Søren Pedersen, Lead Specialist, Radius/Nexel): *"Vi opfordrer til at man tidligt går i dialog med netselskabet omkring projekter, hvor der er behov for større effektmængder. Vi opfordrer også til at udarbejde ladestander strategi så vi som netselskab bedre kan planlægge udbygningen af elnettet."*

For planned larger installations (defined here as **more than 750 amperes**), early dialogue with the grid company is recommended; expect long delivery times, and it's essential to map out the ultimate supply scope early to understand cost implications.

**Connection process duration** depends heavily on available capacity at the site; illustrated via best-case/worst-case scenarios (source table, distribution-level vs. transmission-level reinforcement needs):

| Process phase | Distribution level — Min (months) | Distribution level — Max (months) | Transmission level — Min (months) | Transmission level — Max (months) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Screening | 0.5 | 0.5 | 0.5 | 0.5 |
| Investigation — phase 1 | 1 | 2 | 1 | 2 |
| Investigation — phase 2 / Technical clarification | 1 | 6 | 3 | 6 |
| Project maturation and construction phase | 3 | 24 | 6 | 18 |
| Construction phase (transmission) | — | — | 12 | 36 |
| Commissioning and testing | 3 | 12 | 3 | 12 |
| **Total** | **9 months** | **3 years 9 months** | **2 years 2 months** | **6 years 3 months** |

**Fastest case:** grid connection ready in about **9 months**. **Worst case:** can take **more than 6 years**.

### Charging Infrastructure and Grid-Connected Batteries
Grid expansion is costly, especially at local level where capacity constraints can be severe, requiring careful prioritization of where/when expansion occurs. Grid-connected battery storage — currently still a significantly cost-adding measure — can offer a range of benefits for balancing consumption alongside power production/purchase:
- Reduced connection-size need (fewer amperes), since the battery acts as an energy reservoir
- Better ability to optimize the timing of buying/selling power on the grid (arbitrage)
- Ability to store locally produced power (e.g., from solar panels) for use when most optimal
- Ability to sell flexibility into the ancillary/system-services market

**Systemydelser (Ancillary/System Services):** purchased by **Energinet** (Danish TSO) to ensure stable, secure grid operation in a system increasingly reliant on renewable (primarily solar and wind) generation. Bidding into ancillary services can be done via a private aggregator company handling the service delivery, or internally if the entity has sufficient own capacity. Various service types exist depending on resource type, scale, and geographic location; the business case differs significantly depending on where batteries sit physically in the grid (**DK1** or **DK2** price zones). The report notes the European (and Nordic in particular) electricity market is rapidly changing, requiring close attention to settlement models and expected revenue. The report does not detail Energinet's specific ancillary-service products, but points to Energinet's own website for an overview, and references case studies on batteries published by Energinet: https://energinet.dk/media/efygx5ym/case_batterier.pdf.

### V2G (Vehicle-to-Grid) Possibilities
**V2G** enables two-way interaction between EV batteries and the electricity grid, using stored battery power to help grid operators with balancing and "peak-shaving," similarly to grid-connected batteries but using the RCV fleet's own batteries as the storage resource instead of separate (often grid-scale) batteries. Benefit for the fleet owner: economic gain from making the electric vehicle available for this purpose. V2G technology has been known for some years and used in several projects in Denmark, e.g. the **Parker project** at **Frederiksberg Forsyning**. Historically limited to the **CHAdeMO** standard, not CCS/Type 2 — but with ratification of **ISO standard 15118-20 in 2022**, the market opened for bidirectional charging/discharging including grid connection. Products for V2G implementation are expected to reach market **within about a year** of the report's writing (i.e., roughly 2024).

RCVs are considered a particularly attractive segment for V2G, both due to relatively large battery capacity and fleet scale, and due to their unique usage pattern: RCVs are typically used in morning/late-morning hours and sit idle at the depot the rest of the day, evening, and night — a pattern that fits well with V2G, since vehicles can charge overnight (typically cheapest electricity) and be put into service for grid balancing/arbitrage during periods of high grid load and higher electricity prices.

**PVI (on Dennis Eagle chassis)**
- French manufacturer, part of the **Renault Group**, with ~30 years of battery-electric vehicle experience — likely the longest-established supplier in this market. Originally based its electric drivetrain on lead-acid batteries; for roughly the last 10 years has used **lithium batteries**.
- Produces a range of vehicle types: trucks, RCVs, buses, and smaller vans. PVI's RCV — the **C-Less** — is built on **Dennis Eagle (UK)** chassis, imported to Denmark and the Nordics by **Phoenix Danmark**.
- Purpose-built features for waste collection, including a narrow chassis and automatic lift. Charges via **AC at 22 kW** through a standard CEE plug.
- Available in two battery sizes: **165 kWh** and **265 kWh** nominal capacity. Powered by a **103 kW motor (118 kW with booster)**, with a **9-speed ZF gearbox**.

**Emoss (on Mercedes chassis)**
- **Emoss Mobile Systems B.V.**, a small Dutch company specializing in designing/implementing electric drivetrains for various vehicle types. Converts RCVs based on both new and used **Mercedes Econic** chassis, specified and built in close cooperation with individual customers.
- In Denmark, both **Urbaser** and **Meldgaard** have purchased Emoss-converted vehicles; approximately **15–20** such vehicles operate across Danish municipalities.
- In Scandinavia, Emoss partners with **GeesinkNorba**, which also has units operating in Sweden and Norway besides Denmark; GeesinkNorba supplies **e-PTO** (electric power take-off), bodywork, and lifts (manual and automatic) — i.e., all the components fitted atop the chassis after conversion to full electric drive.
- Emoss has **more than 10 years'** experience converting vehicles to electric drivetrains, across RCVs and other vehicle types; vehicles specified per customer requirements and built in close collaboration.
- The report notes that, despite repeated outreach attempts, **Emoss did not respond**, so product data and experience regarding Emoss vehicles in this report come only from the company's own website and from Danish customers Urbaser and Meldgaard, plus GeesinkNorba.

## Guide to Electrifying Waste Collection

The report frames electrifying waste collection as potentially a bigger undertaking than it might initially appear, spanning technical, economic, organizational, and coordination considerations. The guide offers recommendations, structured as a checklist across 7 focus areas (presented as support material for municipalities considering electrification, not a complete/definitive manual):

### 1. Political Clarification and Communication
Ensure political mandate to initiate electrification.
- Electrification currently represents an extra cost to the municipality — clarify politically the extent of municipal financial backing.
- Municipality should work with a **triple bottom line** so environment, working environment, and climate parameters are included in the accounting, covering both staff and citizens.
- Ensure the municipality's electrification initiative is broadly communicated to both citizens and staff.

### 2. Route Analysis
Analyze and optimize routes so as large a share as possible can be run with electric RCVs.
- Initiate a route analysis, potentially with manufacturers — manufacturers hold software tools to support this.
- Manufacturer capacity varies, so analyses with several manufacturers are advisable if this model is chosen.
- Optimize routes on this basis so as much of the route network as possible can run on the chosen electric RCVs — noting **both range and operating time** are essential considerations here.

### 3. In-House vs. Outsourcing
Investigate whether the new sorting law and/or electrification affects the in-house/outsourcing decision.
- List pros/cons of in-housing vs. outsourcing collection in light of the new regulatory framework.
- Assess in-housing/outsourcing in light of the route analysis.
- If outsourcing, consider whether it may be appropriate to split the service into a collection component and a separate charging-infrastructure component.

### 4. Charging Infrastructure
Map short- and long-term charging infrastructure needs.
- Gain an overview of expected supply needs, short and longer term, alongside route analysis and RCV evaluation.
- Contact the grid company as early as possible in the process to discuss supply options at desired locations and the economic implications of scenarios under consideration.
- Once the type of charging infrastructure to be established becomes clear, consider whether other user groups could also use the infrastructure during daytime hours when RCVs are not using the facilities.

### 5. Municipal Charging-Station Strategy
Assess whether/how the municipality's charging-station strategy (ladestanderstrategi) can factor into charging infrastructure planning.
- If a municipal charging-station strategy exists or is being developed, consider whether/how RCV charging infrastructure can be incorporated into it.
- Coordinate and discuss charging-infrastructure needs with other user groups to minimize establishment costs and optimize service.
- Charging-infrastructure requirement specifications and tender conditions have much in common across use cases and can be optimized this way, potentially via a combined tender.

### 6. Tender
Clarify the tender's scope and process.
- Clarify which parts go to tender: is it the whole waste-collection task, or should the municipality retain parts itself.
- If charging infrastructure is separated from the collection tender, consider coordinating it with other municipal charging-infrastructure projects.

### 7. Organizational Implementation
Approach organizational implementation systematically if collection is brought in-house.
- Be aware this is a major operational change when phasing in electric RCVs — consider a phased rollout, e.g. starting with 3–4 vehicles.
- Carefully select driver/staff teams for the first phase — essential to choose people ready for change, potentially enthusiastic ("brænder for") about electric propulsion, who can become good ambassadors — this may be needed.
- Establish feedback mechanisms between staff, management, and suppliers so problems, faults, or inconveniences are addressed promptly and improvement ideas are discussed.

*(The guide's detailed prose sections mirror and expand on the checklist above, covering: Political Clarification and Communication; Route Analysis and Planning — including the distinction between Aktionsradius/range and Aktionstid/operating time, and typical daily distances of 80–100 km with the caveat that many stops (e.g. multi-story housing) increase consumption and reduce effective range, requiring either an extra charging stop or larger batteries; In-House vs. Outsourcing Considerations — flagging that 5–6 year contracts can be challenging given suppliers must amortize relatively expensive electric RCVs over a short period, one reason some municipalities choose to bring collection in-house, with flexibility and longer contracts as key themes; Charging Infrastructure — recommending an open/flexible approach to AC vs. DC in the early planning phase so as not to unduly restrict which vehicles can be bid, and early dialogue with the grid company; Municipal Charging-Station Strategy — referencing the Esbjerg/Vejle bus depot examples as a model worth considering for refuse collection too; Tender structure — noting that combining collection and infrastructure into one tender is the simplest contractually but not necessarily the most economically optimal, suggesting testing both models via market dialogue and an initial tender round; and Organizational Implementation — reiterating the value of a phased rollout, careful staff selection, supplier-provided training (some suppliers include operator training as part of delivery), and feedback mechanisms, given much of the currently available fleet is first-generation technology.)*

## Closing — Present, Future, and Opportunities

### Market Maturation
The market is transitioning from a near-monopoly to one with multiple manufacturers now offering electric RCVs. Niche players drove early development, but major manufacturers are now entering, which the report expects will make a major difference to vehicle pricing as real market competition is established. Major manufacturers draw on and share technology across their broader heavy-vehicle product lines (trucks, buses, etc.), spreading development costs over larger volumes. Parallel is drawn to the bus sector: early zero-emission bus line tenders required a price premium over diesel buses, but the report notes some routes are now bid at prices **on par with or even below** previous diesel bus costs, attributed to operator experience and increased competition among suppliers, together enabling lower prices for transport companies.

### Technology Development
Beyond sharpened competition and operating experience, falling component prices — especially batteries — are also driving price declines. Citing **BloombergNEF** data (Figur 4, "Vægtede gennemsnitspriser (USD) på batterier" / Weighted average battery prices, USD): over the 10-year period of electric RCV production from **2010–2020**, price per kWh fell from **$1,100/kWh to $137/kWh**, an **89% decline**. In 2020, prices were reported **below the symbolic $100/kWh threshold** for batteries used in electric buses in China.

Contrary to expectations, battery prices **rose in 2022 for the first time**, expected to hold through 2023 before beginning to fall again from **2024**. Increasing diversity in battery chemistries affects individual pack pricing, but overall high demand relative to relatively low supply has driven prices up. Noted developments: growing adoption of **LFP (lithium iron phosphate)** cells, roughly 20% cheaper than other chemistries; **sodium-ion (Natrium)** batteries reaching a maturity level potentially usable in EVs, which could add supply and lower prices generally; and continued anticipation of a **solid-state battery** breakthrough, expected to potentially be producible at around **40% of current cell prices**. The report declines to put a specific year on when this "break-even point" will be reached, given the many factors beyond current battery prices at play — but expects further, potentially substantial (perhaps a halving within a foreseeable timeframe) battery-price reductions, which would significantly affect electric RCV pricing and competitiveness.
