## Introduction — Framing
- Many municipalities want to promote the green transition in transport to improve their climate accounting and reduce local particulate emissions, but ask: what scope of action does a municipality actually have to promote EVs and charging infrastructure? What legal, practical, and technical considerations apply?
- **Municipality's roles enumerated (verbatim list):**
  - **Myndighed (authority)** ensuring the ladestanderbekendtgørelsen is followed at new construction in the municipality
  - **Myndighed** granting permits for installing public charge stands
  - **Ejendomsbesidder (property owner)** implementing the Executive Order at its own buildings (town hall, schools, sports facilities, etc.)
  - **Bygherre og ejer (developer and owner)** of public parking facilities, where chargers are installed per the Executive Order
  - **Flådeejer (fleet owner)** converting to electric vehicles
  - **Arbejdsgiver (employer)** offering staff charging (to the extent possible within Kommunalfuldmagten)
  - **Offentlig indkøber (public procurer)** of transport services

## Kommunens Rolle som Myndighed (Municipality's Role as Authority)
- Can promote EV adoption by allocating a sensible number of public parking spaces as charge spaces, and ensuring sensible parking rules and terms for chargers/charging operators.
- **Under current rules, municipalities have no legal basis under Kommunalfuldmagten to co-finance charge stands.** Municipality **can** map demand and designate relevant locations, then tender these to charging operators, who assess and decide whether installation is commercially attractive.
- Practical implication: allocate a reasonable number of spaces at attractive locations — both to incentivize EV switching (easy to find a spot) and to ensure charging is actually possible where needed.
- **Critical: charge-space parking must be reserved for EVs** — otherwise expensive infrastructure gets blocked, discouraging both drivers and operators from investing.
- **Ideal but currently not legally possible:** reserving charge spaces specifically for *actively charging* EVs. **Second-best approach: regulate parking duration sensibly** — e.g., a **3-5 hour limit during daytime hours (08:00-17:00)**, letting EV drivers get a meaningful charge while ensuring rotation so spaces aren't blocked all day by non-charging cars. Spaces can be used overnight by area residents who naturally shouldn't need to move their car in the middle of the night.
- Municipality **may exempt EVs from parking fees/permits**, providing a significant switching incentive.
- Municipality may set requirements on charger **size and design**, but should be cautious — overly specific requirements risk limiting competition to a small number of operators; should research the range of charger types/designs across different providers first.
- **Ladestanderbekendtgørelsen requires municipalities to install chargers at parking areas at municipal buildings and at new construction/renovation — municipalities are obligated to implement the Executive Order by 2025.** Reference: elbilviden.dk (for the Executive Order text and guidance).

## Kommunens Rolle som Flådeejer (Fleet Owner)
- Typical municipal fleet composition: mainly **hjemmeplejebiler** (home-care vehicles), **vej- og parkkøretøjer** (road and park maintenance vehicles), and some **puljebiler** (pool cars for staff meeting travel) — all readily convertible to electric.
- Notes municipalities have had EVs in their fleets for many years, particularly in home care — **early EVs had short range, but because home-care routes are fixed, EVs already worked well as home-care vehicles back in 2015.**
- **Operational advantages of fleet electrification:** less downtime; significant maintenance savings (EVs need essentially no servicing — largely just brake inspection/cleaning); electricity is a much cheaper fuel than diesel/petrol; high visibility value, communicating the municipality's climate profile in the streetscape and potentially inspiring other fleet owners (e.g. local businesses) to follow.
- Municipal fleet typically charges at **dedicated municipal chargers serving only the fleet** — not listed on public charging networks/operator charge-maps.
- If charging infrastructure is fleet-dedicated only, the municipality can often use **cheaper chargers**, since multi-user billing/settlement handling isn't needed.
- **Load balancing ("lastbalancering") recommended:** a system automatically distributing power to individual charge points based on what each vehicle can accept — enabling a smaller, cheaper grid connection than would be needed if all chargers had to deliver full power simultaneously (rarely actually needed).
- **Recommendation: separate vehicle procurement from charging-infrastructure procurement in tenders** — usually different suppliers anyway, and bundling both into one package hampers both competition and transparency.
- Reference: Copenhagen Electric's guide to fleet conversion, at Elbilviden.dk.

## Kommunens Rolle som Arbejdsgiver (Employer)
- As a large employer, the municipality may want to install chargers for EV-driving staff. **Under current rules, no legal basis under Kommunalfuldmagten for the municipality to finance chargers for public use.** Municipality can map its needs and then dialogue with charging operators about their interest in identified locations — potentially getting **free chargers installed for residents' benefit.**

## Kommunens Rolle som Ejendomsbesidder og Bygherre (Property Owner and Developer)
- Municipality typically owns properties where charging is relevant for visitors — e.g. **town halls, culture houses, sports facilities, and public parking facilities** — with obligations here under the Ladestanderbekendtgørelsen.
- **Under current rules, chargers can only be installed for the municipality's own vehicles**, since Kommunalfuldmagten currently gives no basis for financing publicly accessible chargers.
- **Recommendation: design current charger tenders so that charge stands can be opened to the public if/when legislation changes** — noting the **Transportministeriet (Ministry of Transport) was preparing new legislation** expected to improve municipalities' ability to co-finance publicly accessible chargers.
- **Early-stage advisory involvement recommended for new construction:** strongly advises obtaining advice from multiple charging operators **early in the process, before construction begins.** Cites examples of good intentions undermined when operators were only involved once a parking structure was nearly complete — resulting in incorrectly dimensioned/installed supply cables, switchboards, and/or conduit routing. Retrofitting charging infrastructure not planned in from the start of new construction/renovation can cause unnecessary costs.
- **Municipality may not offer free charging** — this would conflict with Kommunalfuldmagten by competing with private providers. Municipality **may** contract a charging operator to run charging at, e.g., a public parking structure, per applicable tender rules.

## Åben Ladeinfrastruktur (Open Charging Infrastructure) — Accessibility Requirements
- Beyond the EU Buildings Directive (implemented in Denmark as the Ladestanderbekendtgørelsen), a further EU directive — implemented in Danish law via **Bekendtgørelse 473 af 8. maj 2018** (Executive Order 473 of 8 May 2018, via Retsinformation) — requires public chargers be accessible **"without a contract."**
- **Two dimensions of the accessibility requirement, per this guidance's interpretation:**
  1. **Practical accessibility:** charging must be activatable/payable via a commonly available payment method with relative ease — e.g., contactless credit-card payment or credit-card payment via an app. **Not acceptable:** requiring prior customer registration with a physical account card mailed by post, or an overly complicated online process that forms a barrier.
  2. **Economic accessibility:** chargers must also be economically accessible — no per-kWh price or minimum price that compromises real accessibility. **As a public authority, a municipality cannot set a specific price requirement.**
- **Indirect accessibility** can be strongly promoted via **roaming requirements** — letting a charger from one provider be activated using cards from other national and/or international providers.

## Afgiftsrefusion (Tax/Duty Refund)
- If the municipality outsources operation of its own (fleet-only) chargers to an operator, part of the **electricity duty ("elafgift")** can be refunded — approximately **halving the electricity cost.**
- **In 2021, the refund rate is 1.1 DKK per kWh** [note: the source text literally states "1,1 kr/kW" (per kW, not per kWh) — this is almost certainly a typo in the source for "kr/kWh," consistent with how electricity duty refunds are normally denominated and with the surrounding text discussing per-kWh electricity costs; reproduced with this flag rather than silently corrected].
- This meaningfully improves operating economics for high-mileage fleets. **Caveat:** municipalities choosing this route should be mindful of added costs for more advanced chargers, metering, billing, and an operator's ongoing service subscription.

## Almindelige Ladestandere (Normal Chargers) — Technical Detail
- Normal charge boxes/stands are for **destination charging** — parking and charging over several hours.
- Technically, these are **not really "charging units"** but advanced, communication-capable outlets — they deliver AC power to the car's onboard charger, which converts it to DC to charge the battery. **The car's internal charger is typically the bottleneck determining charge speed**, not the charge stand.
- Normal chargers typically deliver **11-22 kW**, but many cars can only accept **3.7 kW.** A few models can accept **22 kW on three phases** — the **Renault Zoe** is specifically named as a model often used in public fleets **precisely because** it can charge relatively fast on a standard charger, supporting **2- and 3-shift home-care operation.** More EVs accepting **11 kW** are increasingly reaching market.
- **Sizing guidance:**
  - A single double-outlet stand: allocate **32A on three phases** total supply, giving **22 kW combined output.**
  - At **6 or more outlets**, allocating **16A × 3-phase per outlet** would be over-dimensioned, since it's rare for that many EVs to charge simultaneously at full power. Instead, a **lower per-outlet supply allocation, distributed via load balancing**, is recommended — e.g., a **63A connection shared across 6-10 charge points.**
  - A lower overall delivery scope from the utility reduces the connection fee ("tilslutningsbidrag"), where spare capacity doesn't already exist — yielding a cheaper fleet charging solution.
- **Safety/equipment note:** when procuring charge boxes/stands for own use, be aware some products **lack built-in DC-fault protection.** In that case, a **Type B HPFI relay** (residual-current device) is required in the switchboard to meet safety requirements under the **Installationsbekendtgørelsen** (Installation Executive Order). **A Type B relay can cost as much as the charger itself** — recommendation: require DC protection or an included Type B relay in the purchase price when procuring new charge boxes.

## Hurtigladere (Fast Chargers) — Errand and Transit Charging — Technical/Cost Detail
- Fast and ultra-fast chargers, unlike normal chargers, are **true charging units** that charge the EV battery directly with DC.

**Three charging-level tiers (verbatim):**

### 15-25 kW — "Semihurtig ladning" (Semi-fast charging)
- All cars can accept the full power output across the **20-80% battery range.**
- Delivers approx. **125 km of range per hour of charging.**
- **Relatively cheap: from approx. 50,000 DKK to acquire.**
- Typically existing supply is already present if at a building; otherwise a **25-35A installation** suffices.
- Good cost-effectiveness; well-suited to e.g. a **town hall**, where a visitor can get a meaningful charge during a typical meeting's duration.

### 50 kW — "Hurtigladning" (Fast charging)
- Most cars can accept all or most of this power across the **20-80% range.**
- Delivers approx. **250 km of range per hour of charging.**
- **Available from approx. 200,000 DKK plus VAT**, plus significant installation and connection-fee costs.
- This level was previously used along motorways but is **now considered too slow** for that purpose, and **too expensive** for other purposes (implying it now occupies an awkward middle ground).

### 75-350 kW — "Lynladning til langfart" (Rapid charging for long-distance travel)
- Only **10-30 minutes** of charging needed to reach 80% battery, before continuing the trip.
- **No cars yet on the market can accept 350 kW**, and initially only luxury vehicles will approach that.
- Several **modular ultra-fast charger types available from approx. 200,000 DKK plus VAT** — can start at, e.g., **50-100 kW** and be upgraded later as demand grows. These modular fast chargers are expected to **replace the older 50 kW stand category.**
- **Ultra-fast stations at 175-350 kW represent investments from over 500,000 DKK to over 1 million DKK**, typically requiring a **dedicated transformer.**

## Opsummering (Summary)
- Municipalities generally both want, and have enormous influence over, the local green transition of the transport sector to electric — benefiting climate accounting and reducing particulate emissions for citizens' benefit.
- Many levers available across the municipality's roles (authority, property owner, developer, fleet owner, employer) — these roles can overlap, e.g. when a new parking facility at a town hall needs charge spaces for guests, staff, and the public simultaneously.
- By mapping and planning for citizens' future charging needs, the municipality can support and accelerate the green EV transition locally.
- Municipalities should be generally aware there are **different relevant charging levels for different purposes.**
- Legislation sets requirements both for **preparation and implementation** of infrastructure in construction, and for **accessibility.**
- Closing framing: it's not just about putting EVs on the agenda, but about the municipality managing its many roles so that charging infrastructure — in both establishment and operation — is as favorable as possible for both the municipality and the individual citizen/EV driver.

## Relevance Notes for HDEV/Depot Tool (Claude's own framing, not from source)
- This document is framed around municipal/passenger-EV charging, but several sections transfer directly and usefully to depot/fleet planning: the **fleet-dedicated charger cost-reduction rationale** (cheaper chargers possible when no multi-user billing is needed; load-balancing enabling smaller/cheaper grid connections), the **procurement recommendation to separate vehicle purchase from charging-infrastructure purchase** in tenders, and the **early-involvement-of-charging-operators warning** for new construction (citing real cases of under-dimensioned supply cable/switchboard/conduit when operators were engaged too late) are all directly applicable good-practice patterns for HDEV depot planning.
- The **three-tier charger cost/power table (15-25kW, 50kW, 75-350kW)** with 2021-era Danish DKK pricing is a useful independent cost reference, distinct from (and roughly consistent in order of magnitude with) the normal/fast/rapid charger cost tables seen in the Køge, Esbjerg, and Horsens municipal strategy documents in this knowledge base — cross-referencing these gives a reasonably well-triangulated 2020-2022 Danish public-charger cost baseline, though still firmly in the light-duty/passenger power range (up to 350kW at the very top end), not HD-truck-scale (which typically requires 350kW-1MW+ chargers and dedicated MV connections).
- The **Kommunalfuldmagten legal constraint** (no municipal co-financing of public chargers, no free charging, fleet chargers must stay fleet-dedicated unless outsourced to an operator) is an important recurring Danish regulatory theme across this entire batch of documents (also seen in the Hillerød and Køge strategy documents) — relevant context for understanding the Danish public-sector commercial/legal environment a CPO or depot operator would need to navigate when partnering with a municipality.
