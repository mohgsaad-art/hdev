## What Is "Byggelogistik" (Construction Logistics)?
Defined as encompassing:
- Coordination of deliveries and transport
- Planning of the delivery process before construction starts
- Planning of working hours
- Dedicated resources taking overall responsibility for site logistics
- Good interaction between the actors involved in construction
- Effective communication with shared access to current information

The report uses a zone model (Figur 1, source: MIMIC deliverable 1.1, Linköping, Oct. 2020):
- **Zone 1:** the construction site itself
- **Zone 2:** activities in the immediate vicinity of the site
- **Zone 3:** activities in the surrounding city

Figur 2 (same source) illustrates transport flows of incoming/outgoing materials and waste/reprocessing materials tied to the construction process. Figur 3 ("Spild i byggeriet" / Waste in construction, source: Sitehub.dk) shows the scale of material and labor waste on a construction site (internal site logistics only) — the report states this is "relatively large" but the chart's exact percentage values are **not clearly stated in the extracted text** (chart image, values not transcribable from source text alone).

## Improving Construction Logistics — Key Recommendations
- Plan logistics before construction starts and follow up continuously during the build-out — currently not done sufficiently, per the authors' industry knowledge.
- Effort should be lifted by many parties (strategic, operational, practical levels).
- **Rule of thumb (explicitly stated as approximate):** each reduced km of transport corresponds to roughly **0.8–1.0 kg CO₂** reduction ("en grov tommelfingerregel").
- Coordination between site foremen (sjakformænd), transport managers (kørselsleder) and drivers is critical; should be addressed at management level and given dedicated time at construction meetings.
- Primary responsibility for proposing optimized logistics lies with contractors, craftsmen, and client advisors (bygherrerådgivere).
- Particular focus recommended on **coordinating return transport** (backhaul) with trucks — potential for large effect from relatively modest effort.
- Recommends dialogue between advisors and clients via **Bygherreforeningen** (Danish Association of Construction Clients) and **FRI** (Foreningen af Rådgivende Ingeniører — Association of Consulting Engineers).
- Flag: unplanned/small ad-hoc material orders cause unnecessary transport and CO₂ emissions — should be coordinated/planned in advance for consolidation.
- **Alternative fuels:** Use of green fuels for trucks seen as a significant climate advantage, reducing local air pollution and noise, especially combined with transport optimization. Mobile charging stations at/near construction sites suggested as an option for promoting EV use. The report explicitly states it does **not** go deeper into this topic, as fuel choice does not affect site logistics itself.

## Case Examples

### 1. SiteHub
- Danish company delivering optimized construction logistics solutions; focuses on material optimization, reuse, and reducing unnecessary processes/material consumption.
- Performs systematic waste sorting with measurable, documented effects; systematic process registration means documentation exists without later retrofitting.
- COWI (report author) states it does not know the quantified effect ("COWI kender ikke effekten heraf").
- Case: Holmens Kanal construction site, Copenhagen K — challenge was heavy cyclist/pedestrian traffic and limited space. SiteHub coordinated material/waste handling on behalf of individual contractors, freeing craftsman time and ensuring environmentally correct waste handling.
- Example video referenced: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FoeSXDPNvIw
- SiteHub has also shifted trips away from vans toward bicycle and public transport, especially at sites with parking constraints (interview with Ulrik Branner, SiteHub).

### 2. Input from a Construction Site in Practice — 4700 A/S
- Contractor **4700 A/S** (https://4700.as/) responsible for soil, sewage and concrete work across ten buildings in **Nordhavnen** (Copenhagen), and site logistics management.
- Detailed transport planning; truck selection depends on route and cargo content.
- Drivers instructed to call ahead on approach, especially on first trips of the day; clear instructions on navigating difficult site access (e.g., reversing through one site, using a ramp for another).
- Coordination among all site contractors to ensure enough space for shuttle-running concrete/sand trucks.
- Concrete pour sequencing: pump truck positioned first, then concrete trucks arrive in sequence; concrete trucks must not wait too long or the concrete hardens and becomes unusable — requires precise scheduling.
- Attempted substitution of sand with soil (jord) due to sand shortage, but abandoned because municipal authority approval processing took too long — cited as evidence of need for faster municipal case processing for alternative solutions.

### 3. Vapnagaard (Helsingør) — BUILD Project Case
- Renovation of the public housing area **Vapnagaard** in Helsingør, 2012–2015, used as a case in the BUILD project.
- Scope: over **1,800 apartments** renovated, at a cost of over **800 million DKK**.
- Included: coordination of large material flows, consolidated goods flows (an **off-site consolidation center**), and extensive data collection on individual processes.
- Materials sourced from varying distances: soil handled locally; roof timber (spærtræ) transported between **Padborg** and **Helsingør**; over **20 containers of roof tiles (tegl)** delivered from **Spain**.

Key findings from Vapnagaard:
- Collaboration on transport solutions at the timber merchant (**Johs. Fog**) optimized loads and used ongoing driver–crew contact for optimal placement of goods, reducing internal transport and likely breakage/loss.
- Collaboration on inbound transport (soil, stone, sand, gravel) meant trucks almost never returned empty from home base — typically carrying gravel/stone/sand for the day's tasks. Trucks also used during breaks to move scaffolding/equipment internally between blocks (per interviews with drivers from **CityContainer**).

**Table 5** — Transport data for waste removal/disposal from the site (referenced; underlying figures **not clearly stated in extracted text**, source: BUILD-rapporten).

- Economics was ultimately decisive for carrier selection; transport and climate played only a minor role.
- Because this was a renovation (not new-build), waste removal/mixed material haul-away was greater than would be typical for new construction. Compared to the total estimated km for the whole project, these waste-removal trips constituted **10–12%** — suggesting return capacity was underutilized. The report recommends focusing on utilizing empty return capacity in renovations, where there is significant interplay between removal of old materials and delivery of new materials.

## Post-BUILD Recommendations (Section 6)
Construction projects in dense urban areas create challenges especially regarding transport of both building materials and labor to/from sites, amid existing traffic congestion and vulnerable road users (particularly in denser areas). Recommended mitigations:
- Reduce number of transports to/from site by reducing material flows (e.g., reusing soil) and best-possible consolidation of inbound/outbound material transport, including reducing/avoiding small van deliveries that could be combined with better planning.
- Perform transport during low-traffic time periods (**ydertimer** / off-peak hours), to the extent possible while respecting noise impact on surroundings.
- Recognizes that accident risks to vulnerable road users are also reduced by the above, along with climate impact from smoother-flowing transport.
- Consolidation of building materials from different suppliers, e.g., at an external warehouse or via the largest building supplier, to reduce total transport count — timber merchants noted as having strong competence here.
- Aim to perform necessary transport with **green trucks (electric)** and also investigate sea or rail as alternatives to road transport (footnote: mainly relevant for continuous deliveries and/or large volumes with less need for narrow delivery time windows).

## Appendix A — Optimal Vehicle Use

### Vans (Varebiler) in Construction
- Vans — defined as vehicles on **yellow plates** ("gule plader") or "papegøjeplader" (parrot plates) with a total weight under **3.5 tonnes** used for craft/goods transport — play a special role in construction.
- Many recurring van deliveries are an ingrained part of logistics, as opposed to larger, planned deliveries — resource-intensive; more consolidated delivery could substantially reduce this transport type, but requires flexibility from sender and receiver.
- Consolidated solutions are typical of e-commerce deliveries; a similar trend is desirable for construction but requires dedicated focus.
- Classic example of non-consolidated transport: "here-and-now" deliveries of e.g. plumbing/electrical (vvs- og el-materiel) to the site (footnote examples: Solar Fastbox or Lemvigh-Müller express/night delivery). This creates delivery reliability but reduces detailed planning need at the cost of increasing delivery/trip counts — a negative outcome for project economics and for km driven, energy use, and climate impact.
- **No statistics currently exist** on van driving volume in Denmark. Reasons stated: vans are treated like passenger cars for vehicle inspection purposes, so odometer readings are only taken at the first inspection after **4 years**; vans also can only be assigned to business-register segments with limited certainty, complicating traffic-work accounting.
- **COWI (2022)** analysis for **Vejdirektoratet** (Danish Road Directorate) on e-commerce found approximately **240,000** vehicles on yellow plates in Denmark, with the large majority used across service trades, including construction. (Source cited: "VD: E-handel og Vejtrafikken. COWI 2022".)
- An earlier COWI analysis found that **15,000–20,000 vans** are used for dedicated goods transport.
- A significant share of construction-related van transport is expected to be able to switch to electric within a short time horizon; charging range and access must be accounted for in planning.

### A.1 Trucks in Construction
- Truck transport related to construction/civil works accounts for **almost 50%** of total nationally-transported road freight volumes — the single largest segment in the land transport universe.
- Over the past 10 years, legislative changes allowed increasing total weight of large contractor vehicle combinations from **48 tonnes to 56 tonnes**, enabling a roughly **20% increase in load capacity to 38 tonnes** on largely identical rigs, giving a noticeable reduction in traffic work (total driven km across all vehicles) for many transports.
- Example: had the annual soil volumes for the **Lynetteholmen** project been handled with 48-tonne trucks instead of 56-tonne trucks, driven km would have increased by approximately **20%**.
- Significant empty running (tomkørsel) still occurs with trucks, including in construction/civil works — better coordination for capacity utilization (loads in both directions) is a focus area, requiring both technical solutions (logistics tools) and mindset change.
- Climate impact from trucks (like vans) expected to fall in coming years, both from fewer km driven and growing adoption of non-diesel fuels. Whether the full truck fleet can be electrified remains uncertain, but the report states: **"we expect the major vehicle manufacturers to offer a full range of heavy electric vehicles around year 2027"** (information obtained from **Volvo** and **Scania** — explicitly cited as sourced from these manufacturers, not the authors' own estimate).
