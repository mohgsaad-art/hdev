# Green Urban Logistics — Municipal Initiative Examples

**Source file:** `GrønBylogistik_rapport - final.pdf`
**Category:** Data report / logistics study
**Original language:** Danish (translated to English below; proper nouns kept in Danish)
**One-line summary:** A 2021 Copenhagen Electric (Region Hovedstaden) report presenting 10 concrete municipal initiative examples — with Danish legal/kommunalfuldmagt analysis — for reducing emissions from van and truck traffic in Danish cities.

## Publication Details
- **Publisher:** Copenhagen Electric, part of Region Hovedstaden's effort to be the leading European region on e-mobility by 2030.
- **Published:** 2021.
- **Author:** External consultant **Susanne Krawack**, with legal assistance from **Horten** (law firm). Report notes further legal review may be needed for implementation.
- **Purpose:** Give Danish municipalities (kommuner) 10 concrete initiative proposals to lower barriers to introducing greener urban logistics (bylogistik), always recommending implementation via collaboration/dialogue with affected parties.
- **Geographic scope:** Denmark, with case examples drawn from Sweden (Gothenburg, Stockholm, Södertörn), Netherlands (Utrecht), Norway (Oslo, Trondheim), and Czechia (Prague).

## Context / Introduction
- In most municipal traffic planning, van and truck traffic plays a limited role; most analyses/measures target passenger traffic (largest source of congestion).
- From a climate/environment perspective, van and truck traffic is more significant: it accounts for **approximately one-third (en tredjedel) of CO2 emissions from traffic**, both at city and national level.
- Two initiative types identified to reduce emissions:
  1. Increase vehicle capacity utilization / deliver with vehicles matched to goods volume (e.g., bicycles where possible) — same goods volume delivered with fewer vehicles/km.
  2. Shift vehicle fuels to lower-emission fuels, especially electric vehicles, for long-term energy-efficient transition to zero-emission vehicles.
- Each of the 10 initiatives is described with: a summary, positive/negative effects (for city/surroundings, goods recipients, and transporters — "transportør" used broadly to include logistics chain responsible parties, not just physical carriers), a concrete example, legal assessment under **kommunalfuldmagtsreglerne** (the Danish doctrine of municipal authority — unwritten legal principles governing what tasks municipalities may lawfully undertake, generally requiring a legitimate municipal interest and prohibiting municipalities from engaging in commercial/business activity), effects on traffic/environment, relevant geography, and process/initiator.

## The 10 Initiatives

### 1. Consolidation Center and Distribution on Electric Vehicles
**Summary:** Goods for an area delivered to a consolidation center instead of directly to the customer; goods from multiple carriers packed for the individual recipient and delivered together in one electric vehicle.

**Effects:**
| | Positive | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| City/surroundings | Fewer vans/trucks in dense urban areas; better air quality; less noise; increased safety and better urban environment | Requires space for a transshipment center; resources for facilitation and possibly financial support |
| Goods recipient | Fewer deliveries; better ability to agree concrete delivery terms, better planning/resource use | Delivery becomes more expensive due to extra handling |
| Transporter | Less time in congestion | Loses direct customer contact, risking loss of other logistics services for goods recipients/senders |

**Example — Stadsleveransen, Gothenburg (Sweden):**
- Most well-functioning example. Pedestrian-street shops changed their delivery address to the transshipment center; parcels consolidated and delivered by electric vehicle.
- Initiated by the municipality via EU projects; started with a small area/few shops and grew.
- Incentive structure: shorter delivery time window for fossil vehicles in the pedestrian street; free delivery time for electric vehicles/cargo bikes. Fossil vehicles still allowed for full-pallet deliveries.
- Started **2012**; by report date, approx. **500 shops** participate, approx. **800 parcels/day** distributed, operated by **3 electric vehicles and 2 cargo bikes (ladcykler)**. **PostNord** and **DHL** use the scheme for parcel distribution in the area.
- Return goods (waste/emballage) previously handled in the scheme but stopped due to lack of economics.
- Transshipment facility financed by municipality and EU projects; operations financed by carriers and advertising revenue. Goal was for the scheme to be self-sustaining from 2017, not fully achieved. In **2020**, operations taken over by a private parcel distribution company via a tender. Previously operated by "Innerstaden," an association of involved local businesses.
- Other examples: several Dutch cities have similar transshipment centers, but typically only a small share of city goods actually passes through them. In Denmark, **Citylogistik A/S** provides this service; typically needs the municipal town hall as a customer for sufficient volume. **Roskilde Kommune** experience: the scheme freed up resources from parcel-handling tasks; parcels arrive less often and more consolidated.

**Legal assessment:** Under current legislation, municipalities cannot own or operate transshipment centers (would constitute unlawful business activity); cannot tender the operation, not even as a concession. Municipalities CAN facilitate the process (e.g., hosting information meetings, bringing actors together) and CAN provide earmarked support (øremærket støtte) if kommunalfuldmagt conditions are met — support must be earmarked for lawful municipal purposes (infrastructure, traffic flow, city appearance, environment). Municipalities cannot provide free space/land for the project (support for premises/land cannot be earmarked). Earmarked support requires municipal oversight of use and limits on recipient profit. If support affects trade, must comply with **EU state aid rules**, notably the **de minimis rule** (municipality can grant a single business up to approx. **1.5 million DKK** over a rolling 3 fiscal years) and the **group exemption regulation** (gruppefritagelsesforordningen). Municipalities also have statutory authority under **Miljøbeskyttelseslovens § 15 a** (Environmental Protection Act) and **Færdselslovens § 92 d** (Road Traffic Act) to establish environmental zones and time/geographically limited traffic-regulation trials — cannot alone provide sufficient legal basis for establishing/operating a transshipment center, but can authorize measures promoting EV deliveries from one. Municipalities may lease at market price with proper authority and a weighty municipal interest.

**Effect on traffic/environment:** Goods traffic reduced by **70%** in the area covered by the scheme (measured reduction). Because the covered area is very small, overall effect on Gothenburg's total van/truck traffic is limited. CO2 and air pollution reduced, but only locally within the pedestrian-street network.

**Relevant geography:** Best suited to smaller geographic areas with many small shipments from many different producers; less benefit where chain stores dominate (already well-consolidated logistics chains); best for areas with many individual shops buying smaller quantities from diverse producers.

**Process:** Typically municipality-initiated to pacify central city areas; municipality contributes at minimum resources to define/establish the scheme (legal help, engaging participants). Gothenburg experience shows a long-term commitment is required to reach market viability; many similar initiatives start with project funding and stop when that funding (typically after a couple of years) ends.

### 2. Consolidated Delivery to Various Public Actors and Distribution on Electric Vehicles
**Summary:** Coordination of deliveries to various public actors; joint procurement system and better organization of deliveries to individual institutions through a transshipment center where goods are packed jointly per institution.

**Effects:**
| | Positive | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| City/surroundings | Fewer trucks/vans in the area; lower emissions | Requires internal resources to organize |
| Goods recipient | Receives goods jointly, less often; more influence over delivery timing → better planning/resource use | Less self-determination in purchasing |
| Transporter | One carrier wins the whole tender | Smaller market for other carriers; risk of losing other logistics services; increased vehicle requirements |

**Example — Joint delivery in Södertörn (Sweden):**
- **8 municipalities** south of Stockholm coordinate procurement; goods delivered to one warehouse, then distributed jointly to municipal institutions.
- Started **2014** after a few years of preparation; small joint organization established among the 8 municipalities; a joint procurement manual developed. Not all municipal purchasing is included in the joint scheme. Initial phase focused primarily on institutional kitchens (meals for children and elderly).
- The 8 municipalities together have approx. **500,000 inhabitants** and an area of **1,200 km²**.
- Warehouse operation and distribution to municipal institutions outsourced (udliciteret) for **4-year periods**; tenders require environmentally friendly vehicles.
- Other benefits: goods delivered jointly at agreed times, requiring fewer staff resources to handle; fewer vans/trucks at municipal institutions (schools, kindergartens, elderly centers) → better traffic safety.

**Legal assessment:** A municipality can, under the kommunalfuldmagt principle of "production for own use," establish a scheme for joint coordination of deliveries to different municipal institutions; may outsource warehouse/distribution operation to a private actor. Cross-municipal joint schemes are also lawful, e.g., via a joint legal entity (fælles aktieselskab, where no single municipality has controlling influence) or a "kommunalt fællesskab" under **den kommunale styrelseslov § 60** (Local Government Act), typically established as a partnership (interessentskab). Collaboration without a joint legal entity is possible if it's a genuine reciprocal arrangement, or under **lov nr. 548** if services stay below the tender threshold value.

**Effect on traffic/environment:** Evaluated by **IVL Svenska Miljöinstituttet in 2017**. Comparing traffic/emissions between the transshipment center and customer before vs. after the scheme was introduced: emissions reduced by approx. **70%**. Comparing pure transport volume before/after: reduced by approx. **7%** — the rest of the reduction attributable to fleet emission requirements (carriers required to use low-emission fuels — currently **Biogas** and **HVO** used on a large share of trips), reducing CO2. Notes this improvement also reflects broader fleet-emission trends across the sector in the same period. Green fuels also reduce air pollution (particulates especially reduced with HVO/biogas), though NOx emissions in street spaces remain.

**Relevant geography:** Broadly applicable; potential linked more to municipalities that don't currently coordinate procurement or have joint purchasing agreements, whether across institutions within large municipalities or across smaller municipalities in cooperation.

**Process:** Typically one or more municipalities initiate a joint procurement scheme, often part of broader inter-municipal cooperation.

### 3. Municipal Green Procurement Policy
**Summary:** Municipalities, as large "businesses," can use their purchasing power to require delivery of goods/services to the municipality using environmentally/climate-friendly vehicles.

**Effects:**
| | Positive | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| City/surroundings | Fewer fossil vans/trucks in the city, less local air pollution, increased safety, attractive urban environment | Resources needed to organize procurement work, market dialogues, etc. |
| Goods recipient | Less noise/air pollution at delivery | Potentially more expensive delivery |
| Transporter | Clear, uniform framework | Requirement for new vehicles |

**Example:** Concrete examples of green procurement policy cited in **Copenhagen (København)** and **Aarhus** municipalities.
- **POGI** (Partnerskab for Offentlige Grønne Indkøb — Partnership for Public Green Procurement), an initiative driven by **Miljøstyrelsen** (Danish Environmental Protection Agency), works for uniform green procurement guidelines across municipalities and can support municipalities in this work.
- Municipalities often assign EV delivery a scored value for an initial 2–3-year period in tender evaluations (alongside price and other sustainability criteria), later requiring EV delivery outright after some years.

**Legal assessment:** Municipalities may lawfully require electric-vehicle delivery in procurement if this serves a legitimate municipal interest and is proportionate to that interest (proportionality between municipal interest and cost). Established practice from municipal supervisory authorities (det kommunale tilsyn) confirms environmental considerations are a lawful municipal interest; municipalities also have a lawful interest in acting as role models in procurement (per the so-called **Fair Trade-udtalelsen** / Fair Trade opinion). Under procurement law (udbudsretligt), requirements must comply with the equal-treatment principle and be proportionate, and must relate to the subject matter of the contract — generally satisfied when requiring EV delivery of goods/services. Municipalities often conduct market dialogue before tenders to confirm sufficient supplier capability for EV delivery.

**Effect on traffic/environment:** The share of deliveries by EV naturally reduces CO2, air pollution and noise; effect accrues gradually as more tenders are actually delivered with green vehicles. **Neither Copenhagen nor Aarhus** has conducted an assessment of the scale of these effects (explicitly stated as not evaluated).

**Relevant geography:** Applicable everywhere; greatest sense in requiring green delivery for the last leg into larger cities, since most carriers consolidate goods near larger cities.

**Process:** Municipality adopts a procurement policy setting vehicle requirements and rollout timeline; municipal procurement department implements and dialogues with the market. POGI supports with suggested requirements but is also a consensus body, so requirements often reflect the lowest common denominator.

### 4. Green Construction Logistics
**Summary:** Coordination, transshipment, and delivery of construction materials with environmentally/climate-friendly vehicles to construction sites would significantly reduce van/truck traffic and emissions to/from construction sites.

**Effects:**
| | Positive | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| City/surroundings | Fewer vans/trucks near construction activity, less local air pollution, safer/more attractive urban environment | Requires space for transshipment center |
| Goods recipient | Fewer deliveries to the site; faster construction process; potential large savings from resource optimization | Resources needed to organize procurement process |
| Transporter | Easier delivery to a transshipment center | Less transport and fewer rush deliveries |

**Example — Construction logistics in Utrecht (Netherlands):**
- Background: municipality's concern that renovation of the central railway station would cause total traffic chaos due to heavy truck traffic to the site.
- Municipality engaged in dialogue with developers; established a warehouse (**Byggelogistisk Center** / Construction Logistics Center) near the site where all deliveries were received; materials transported jointly to the site on electric vehicles wherever possible. Personnel also reported to the Center and were transported to the site by electric vehicle.
- Not a formal regulation; municipality's role was largely facilitating.
- **2010–2012:** project planned; market dialogue with construction partners; suitable site found for the Center; logistics software developed to handle all goods in one system — all preparations carried out by the municipality.
- **2012–2018:** logistics center operated during construction, open **24/7**; all site deliveries routed through the center. Financing was part of the construction project, covered by the developer (bygherre) and carrier.
- **Other example — Oslo:** Decided to establish **emission-free construction sites** (from 2025 for the municipality's own construction), with intention to later make this a condition in all building permits.

**Legal assessment:** Municipalities have a lawful interest in infrastructure, traffic flow, city appearance and environment, allowing some resource use to facilitate green construction logistics (e.g., a transshipment center), subject to proportionality between resource use and municipal interest, and ensuring involvement does not constitute state aid to individual businesses — meaning a municipality **cannot finance** a construction logistics center itself; it must be financed by e.g. developers or suppliers. Under current building regulations (**BR18**), municipalities cannot impose requirements promoting sustainable construction (incl. emission-free sites) in building permits generally — but CAN set such requirements where the municipality itself is the developer (bygherre).

**Effect on traffic/environment:** Construction-related truck traffic constitutes roughly one-third of CO2 emissions from truck traffic in several larger cities, including Utrecht. Registered number of transports to the site was reduced by **70%** relative to the expected number. Personal traffic (personal vehicle trips) to the site reduced by **40%**. Capacity utilization on trucks between the logistics center and site was **90%**, saving **1 hour 20 minutes per transport**. Construction itself finished faster than planned, likely due to greater focus on having materials ready when needed.

**Relevant geography:** Potentially applicable anywhere, but greatest potential in larger urban areas where both emissions and congestion can be reduced.

**Process:** Municipality drove the process in Utrecht despite reluctance from the construction industry; not adopted by industry itself. Considered best achieved via general legislation or specific municipal requirements.

### 5. Green Operations and Civil Works (Grøn drift og anlægsarbejde)
**Summary:** Municipal air pollution, CO2, and noise emissions can potentially be reduced in civil works and in operation/maintenance of municipal land, by procuring/leasing environmentally/climate-friendly equipment and requiring green equipment in tenders.

**Effects:**
| | Positive | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| City/surroundings | Less emissions and noise, less local air pollution | Potentially more expensive construction and operations |

**Example — Copenhagen Municipality (Københavns Kommune):**
- Intention to reduce CO2 emissions from work machinery by **30–40%** in the period **2021–2025**.
- Estimated emissions from work machinery: approx. **75,000 tonnes CO2/year** in Copenhagen.
- Electric machinery generally used for smaller machines under **2.5 tonnes**; for larger machines without electric solutions, sustainable **HVO (Hydrogenereret Vegetabilsk Olie / Hydrogenated Vegetable Oil)** used.
- **10 pilot projects** initiated in 2020–2021; by mid-2021, approximately **15** construction/civil works projects underway.
- **0.5 million DKK** allocated respectively to construction and civil works budgets to co-finance higher costs of green construction/civil works.
- Municipality requires tenders to quote both a fossil/emission-free machinery solution price and a conventional machinery price, then chooses where to apply pool funds to enable fossil-free works.
- **Increased costs** for projects using fossil/emission-free machinery: approx. **2–5%** of the construction sum vs. conventional machinery. Large machines, e.g. **25-tonne excavators**, can cost up to **2–3 times more** than equivalent conventional machines.
- A "Samarbejdsforum for fossil- og emissionsfrie arbejdsmaskiner" (Collaboration Forum for Fossil- and Emission-Free Work Machines) established, involving public buyers, contractors, and machine rental companies to discuss the market and tender criteria.
- **Other examples:**
  - **Vejdirektoratet** (Danish Road Directorate) has begun requiring lower-emission equipment for state road operation/maintenance, but specific requirements/effects not yet published as of report date.
  - **Frederiksberg Kommune** has used smaller electric vehicles for operations/maintenance since **2010**, assessed to have worked well, reducing both noise and emissions.
  - **Oslo Kommune** has worked since **2015** to establish fossil-free construction sites for its own construction, reporting both contractors and area residents are highly satisfied.

**Legal assessment:** As environmental concern is a lawful municipal interest, municipalities may under kommunalfuldmagtsreglerne procure electric equipment for own tasks (e.g., road/park work), even if more expensive than alternatives, provided proportionality between municipal interest and any price premium is maintained. Same applies to joint municipal cooperatives. Requiring electric equipment in procurement is similarly lawful, subject to procurement law's equal-treatment and proportionality principles and relevance to contract subject matter.

**Effect on traffic/environment:** A comprehensive assessment of climate/environment effects of fossil- and emission-free civil works in Copenhagen is underway (not yet complete per report). In all cases: substantial CO2 reductions of **80–100%**, reduced air pollution (approx. **25% with biofuel, 100% with electric**), and noise reduction — scope depends on the specific machinery mix in each civil works project.

**Relevant geography:** Not specifically limited; applies to municipal operations generally.

**Process:** Municipality initiates by deciding to reduce emissions from civil works and land operations; applies to own equipment and to procurement; funds should be allocated for potentially higher-cost construction/operations during a transition period.

### 6. Delivery in Off-Peak Hours (Levering i ydertimer)
**Summary:** Goods can be delivered outside rush hours, reducing congestion and carrier time loss in traffic. Usually combined with a requirement for noise-free (støjfri) delivery.

**Effects:**
| | Positive | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| City/surroundings | Fewer trucks in rush-hour traffic, less congestion, better urban environment | Possible noise issues during delivery/goods handling |
| Goods recipient | Goods arrive before shops open | Must be prepared for noise-reduced delivery, potentially requiring investment |
| Transporter | Less wasted time in traffic; better equipment utilization | Night work; special vehicle/handling requirements |

**Details:**
- Mainly a carrier-driven wish (typically supermarket/large-unit delivery), requiring the recipient to have capability to secure access and prepare the goods area.
- Main argument against: noise nuisance to nearby residents, especially problematic at night.
- Initiative: municipality agrees with carriers on delivery time windows if delivery is done in a noise-reduced (støjsvag) manner.
- **Miljøstyrelsen** (Danish EPA) has published guidance on reducing noise nuisance from goods handling, including recommended distances between goods handling and residences — based on the **Dutch PEAK certification** for equipment. Reference: **Miljøstyrelsens vejledning 32 (2019)**.
- Noise from goods handling is regulated under **Miljøbeskyttelsesloven** (Environmental Protection Act) — nearby residents can lodge noise complaints, and the municipality must determine if the nuisance is significant.

**Example — Schulstad delivery:**
- **Schulstad** delivered bread to supermarkets in **Københavns Kommune** before 7:00 AM, using **hybrid vehicles** that run electric under 30 km/h, with otherwise noise-reduced delivery (e.g., reversing alarm replaced with a camera). Schulstad also invested in especially noise-reduced loading equipment, and drivers trained in noise-reduced behavior (no car radio or mobile phone during work). Trial was successful for Schulstad — saved time by avoiding rush hour, offsetting the investment in noise-reduced equipment.

**Legal assessment:** Legal framework depends on whether delivery is to the municipality itself (municipal institutions) or to private entities (supermarkets etc.). As purchaser, the municipality may accept off-peak delivery when compliant with noise requirements; if off-peak delivery is at the supplier's request, it should not raise municipal costs, but where there is a legitimate, proportional municipal interest (e.g., inner-city traffic-flow considerations), the municipality can bear additional costs. For deliveries to private parties, municipalities can influence via planning (kommuneplan/lokalplan — locating new shops to reduce/avoid noise nuisance) and via enforcement orders under **Miljøbeskyttelseslovens § 42** regarding noise limits (e.g., requiring shielding or quiet equipment). See also **Miljøstyrelsens vejledning nr. 32 (2019)**.

**Effect on traffic/environment:** Off-peak delivery reduces truck traffic during rush hour, when many commuter cyclists are present. No large-scale Danish off-peak delivery schemes exist yet that show up in aggregate truck traffic statistics.
- **Stockholm pilot:** tested night delivery vs. day delivery to **3 shops**. Effect: deliveries performed faster and used less fuel, but large variation between the 3 delivery sites due to individual site conditions.
- **Trafikstyrelsen** found Danish night-delivery trials led to a fuel consumption reduction of **7–8%**. Smaller climate/environment effect attributed to the fact that the same transports are performed — the effect stems mainly from smoother driving; time savings (and thus economic benefit) are higher than the climate effect.

**Relevant geography:** Most relevant where there are significant daytime congestion problems and daily delivery is needed before shop opening (e.g., before 9 AM); also relevant near school routes where heavy vehicles during rush hour are undesirable.

**Process:** Initiative often taken by carriers wanting off-peak delivery; municipality can also initiate to limit heavy traffic. Both parties must agree terms/conditions. Miljøstyrelsen's guidance provides a basis for agreements/guidelines on noise-reduced delivery.

### 7. Coordinated Goods Delivery and Waste Collection
**Summary:** Coordination of different transport types for better vehicle utilization — primarily by taking return goods and clean waste fractions back when delivering goods.

**Effects:**
| | Positive | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| City/surroundings | Fewer and smaller vehicles in the city; less local air pollution, safer/more attractive urban environment | Space needed for a transshipment center |
| Goods recipient | Carrier can take return goods, waste/packaging back with goods delivery; operational optimization, resource savings | Potentially more expensive delivery |
| Transporter | | Risk of reduced competition if handled by a single operator; risk of losing other logistics services for recipients/senders |

**Example — Älskade Stad ("Beloved City"), Stockholm:**
- Concept now also used in other Swedish cities and in **Oslo** and **Trondheim**.
- Core idea: use the same vehicles for parcel delivery and waste collection in dense urban areas — large waste vehicles follow the same routes as parcel distributors; as waste fractions increase (more categories collected), some (e.g., cardboard/plastic packaging) can be collected on smaller vehicles.
- A transshipment center consolidates parcels and transfers to smaller electric vehicles; these vehicles also transport some waste fractions back to the terminal, from where it's consolidated and transported on larger vehicles to waste treatment. Smaller EVs mean shorter routes; collection from these is consolidated at the transshipment center.
- Area: mixed office buildings and apartments. Run in collaboration between waste company **Ragn-Sells**, distributor **Bring**, and large property manager **Vasakronan** — three companies jointly operate the concept; Stockholm municipality supports the project but did not take an active initiating role.
- Initiative taken by the property company and waste company; concept suits areas with a large property company able to help organize it and with a suitable transshipment site; the property company can also use vehicles for facility management/property service.
- Requires no special regulation, but requires physical space for a transshipment center.

**Legal assessment:** Similar to Initiative 1 — municipalities cannot establish/operate the scheme or provide municipal land under kommunalfuldmagtsreglerne (would constitute unauthorized business activity), but can provide earmarked support for lawful municipal interests (infrastructure, traffic flow, city appearance, environment) and facilitate the project, subject to oversight and EU state aid rules (de minimis, group exemption regulation) where trade is affected. Schemes involving waste return must respect that municipalities cannot set rules for or handle source-separated recyclable commercial/business waste.

**Effect on traffic/environment:** Fewer vehicles needed since loads run both directions; shift from large waste vehicles to smaller electric vehicles. Reduction in both air pollution and CO2. For waste transport specifically: a **markant CO2 reduction of approx. 75%, from 50% down to 13.4 g CO2 per kg of waste**, compared to the conventional model using diesel waste vehicles (operational emissions only; excludes lifecycle effects e.g. vehicle production). An evaluation of the trial assessed the system as economically sound but the project as still too small/hands-on to see the full potential.

**Relevant geography:** Best suited to dense areas with offices and residences; requires a limited number of actors for easy coordination. Potential to remove large heavy waste vehicles from dense urban areas. Involving multiple parcel carriers is desirable but collaboration between carriers is often difficult — cited as likely reason only **Bring** participates in Älskade Stad.

**Process:** Typically initiated by whoever collects commercial waste (especially clean fractions) together with property administration — often a service to office businesses that struggle to have multi-fraction waste collected. Municipality can help by bringing relevant parties together and presenting the idea; dedicating an area for transshipment assessed as positively contributing to establishment.

### 8. Loading Areas with Fast Chargers
**Summary:** As an incentive to switch to electric vans/trucks, parking spaces with charging infrastructure for vans/trucks would be attractive — but must be well-located to make sense in a tight logistics chain.

**Effects:**
| | Positive | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| City/surroundings | Potential for less-emitting van/truck traffic and better urban environment | Parking must be reserved and charging infrastructure agreements made |
| Goods recipient | Potential EV delivery; greener profile; better urban environment for residents/shoppers | — |
| Transporter | Easier parking and charging | Possible costs of switching to EVs |

**Details:**
- No concrete examples of an established larger network of reserved parking with charging infrastructure exist — no concrete experience to reference (explicitly stated).
- Municipality could designate parking for electric vans/trucks with charging infrastructure, potentially combined with restrictions limiting parking to EVs only; fast chargers needed since most park for short periods.
- Municipality can mark special parking areas with stopping bans for other vehicle types, within relevant time intervals, but **cannot itself establish charging infrastructure**.
- Charging infrastructure needs for vans/trucks could be included in a municipal siting plan to support businesses that do establish charging infrastructure.
- **Estimated charging location split (stated as an expectation, not measured):** approx. **80%** of distribution-vehicle charging expected to occur at the distribution company's own warehouse/depot; a smaller share along the main road network; only approx. **6%** expected to need charging on public land in urban areas. Report recommends a market survey of distribution companies' assessment of urban charging need.

**Legal assessment:** Under **Vejlovens § 92** (Road Act), with police consent, municipalities can reserve parking spaces for electric vehicles for environmental reasons — assessed as allowing a "general reservation" of parking for defined EV types (e.g., trucks) on environmental grounds. Municipalities currently have **no legal authority to establish publicly accessible EV charging stations** (cf. **Ankestyrelsens udtalelse af 27. januar 2021 til Frederiksberg Kommune** — Danish National Social Appeals Board ruling to Frederiksberg Municipality). Since municipalities cannot do this themselves, they also cannot tender the task to private actors, not even as a concession. Municipalities CAN establish charging stations for the municipality's own use, and may sell incidental surplus capacity at market price if the surplus arose coincidentally and cannot be eliminated. New legislation expected following the **Eldrup Kommissionens delrapport II, February 2021** ("Veje til en veludbygget ladeinfrastruktur" / "Paths to a well-developed charging infrastructure"), recommending municipalities get authority to contribute to market-based charging infrastructure rollout — as road authority (kerbside charging on public land), as public employer (workplace charging), via implementation of the **ladestanderbekendtgørelsen** (charging station executive order — pre-installation requirements in new construction parking), and via local/municipal planning (housing associations, shopping centers, public parking near halls, etc.).

**Effect on traffic/environment:** No dedicated parking with charging infrastructure has been established; assessed as potentially supportive of EV-switch decisions but effect cannot be quantified. Considered relatively easy for a municipality to introduce, primarily requiring planning; mainly a "nudge" initiative.

**Relevant geography:** Relevant only in cities where finding space for unloading goods is difficult and where e.g. craftsmen struggle to find parking while working in dense city areas.

**Process:** Municipality initiates as part of its parking strategy.

### 9. Zero-Emission Zones
**Summary:** Zones can be established where only zero-emission vehicles may drive — functioning similarly to existing Danish environmental zones (miljøzoner) in the largest cities.

**Effects:**
| | Positive | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| City/surroundings | Reduced noise, air pollution, better urban environment | Costs for carriers/citizens; resources for planning and administration |
| Goods recipient | Better local environment | Potentially more expensive delivery |
| Transporter | Equal conditions for all carriers | Investment in new vehicles |

**Details:**
- On the agenda in European cities; expected to be established in the **Netherlands** and **Norway in 2025**.
- In Denmark, the government's green cities proposal of **26 May 2021** opened the possibility for municipalities to establish zero-emission zones (framework not yet specified). Also announced that existing environmental zones could be tightened so all diesel vehicles without particulate filters are barred. Environmental zones (miljøzoner) currently exist in **the 4 largest Danish cities**.
- Not yet implemented anywhere per report date; Netherlands finalizing legislation, intending **30–40 cities** with zero-emission zones by **2025**; state support for EV purchases exists there. In Netherlands, intention is to start with vans/trucks, later extending to passenger cars.
- Regulation could mirror environmental zones: license plate reading and automatic vehicle-register checks determine access rights; automatic fines issued for violations.

**Legal assessment:** No current legislation enables municipalities to establish zero-emission zones in defined urban areas; new legislation required (also for trial schemes). As of spring 2021, a parliamentary resolution proposal (folketingsbeslutning) had been introduced to urgently grant municipal authority both for tightened environmental zones for passenger cars and for establishing zero-emission zones in city districts. **Transportministeriet**, **Miljøministeriet**, and **Københavns Kommune** (part of a climate cooperation agreement on green public transport, signed **25 June 2020**) are investigating a legislative process enabling zero-emission zones as a trial scheme.

**Effect on traffic/environment:** No concrete experience exists yet (not implemented anywhere). **Illustrative calculation example (explicitly a hypothetical, not a measured result):** if a zero-emission zone for vans/trucks in Copenhagen's Indre By (Inner City) reduced 90% of CO2 emissions from van/truck traffic there, this would equate to eliminating **16%** of van/truck traffic emissions municipality-wide, corresponding to a **4%** reduction in total CO2 emissions from road traffic in Copenhagen. Effects would not be confined to the zone itself, since vehicles do not change mode at the zone boundary — driving outside the zone with zero-emission vehicles would also contribute reductions over a larger area.

**Relevant geography:** Especially relevant in city centers where noise and air pollution affect many people; effectiveness depends on zone size (larger zones create stronger switching incentives) and requires dialogue with the transport industry on scope.

**Process:** Initiative taken by the municipality in close dialogue with affected groups; if the zone covers only vans/trucks, involving the transport industry in planning is essential. Framework conditions must be established before implementation in Danish cities.

### 10. Transshipment to Bicycle (Omladning til cykel)
**Summary:** Transshipping to bicycles for the last stretch of delivery is in demand from several carriers — faster and results in lower urban emissions.

**Effects:**
| | Positive | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| City/surroundings | Less emissions in the city, safer/more attractive urban environment | Space needed for a transshipment point |
| Goods recipient | Fast deliveries | May receive goods in multiple batches |
| Transporter | Moves through traffic faster | More expensive delivery due to extra transshipment step |

**Examples:**
- **DHL:** established a bike transshipment point on **Nørre Voldgade, Copenhagen** in **2019**. From this center, deliveries to the **Middelalderbyen** (medieval city center) made by electric cargo bikes (el-ladcykler). Goods delivered to the transshipment center by van/truck a few times daily; parcels from Middelalderbyen also collected there and delivered by vehicle to other destinations. Plans exist to deliver directly from the airport to the Copenhagen transshipment center using electric vehicles.
- **Velopak (Aarhus):** delivering goods by bicycle since **2011**. **12 cargo-bike couriers (cykelbude)**, using different bike types depending on the shipment, operating within the **Ringvejen** (ring road) in Aarhus, delivering goods loads up to **280 kg** using a trailer behind the bike. Mostly used for smaller shipments (parcels, food, letters) to addresses in the dense part of the city where bikes have an accessibility advantage.
- **Prague:** area under a bridge used for bike transshipment; municipality made the area available to all companies wanting to transport by bike, fenced (murerhegn) so bikes can be locked securely overnight; functions as a transshipment center during day/evening hours where bikes collect/deliver parcels, vans fill/empty the area.

**Legal assessment:** Municipalities cannot under kommunalfuldmagtsreglerne establish, operate, or provide municipal land for a bike transshipment point for private businesses (would be either unauthorized business activity or state aid). Municipalities CAN provide earmarked support for lawful municipal interests linked to bike transshipment (infrastructure, traffic flow, city appearance, environment), subject to oversight and profit-limitation conditions on recipients, and EU state aid rules (de minimis, group exemption regulation) where trade is affected. As in Initiative 1, statutory authority under **Miljøbeskyttelseslovens § 15 a** and **Færdselslovens § 92 d** allows environmental zones and limited traffic-regulation trials that could promote e-cargo-bike deliveries from a transshipment center.

**Effect on traffic/environment:** No aggregate figures exist for how much CO2 bike delivery saves a city (explicitly stated). **Velopak** reports delivering approx. **25,000 parcels/year** and driving **40,000 km/year** across the company's 12 bikes — but this cannot be directly converted to reduced driving or CO2 (explicitly stated as not directly convertible). Clear qualitative reduction in both dense-area traffic and air pollution/CO2 is asserted without a specific figure. **DHL** cargo bikes are part of a global ambition to reduce CO2 emissions by **50% by 2025** (vs. 2007 baseline) and achieve **zero emissions by 2050**.

**Relevant geography:** Primarily relevant in cities offering the dual benefit of faster movement through congestion and reduced emissions; not limited to very dense areas — mixed urban areas can also benefit; finding a suitable transshipment site may be easier slightly outside the dense city core.

**Process:** Historically initiated by suppliers/carriers; municipality can support by demanding bike delivery for its own goods orders, designating suitable transshipment areas at a convenient distance from the dense city, or leasing/supporting municipal land during a trial period (as in Initiative 1).
