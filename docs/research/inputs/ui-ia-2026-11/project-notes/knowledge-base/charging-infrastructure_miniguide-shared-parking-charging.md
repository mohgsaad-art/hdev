# Miniguide: Charging Infrastructure for Residential Buildings with Shared Parking Areas

**Source file:** `Miniguide-Ladeinfrastruktur-til-beboelsesbygninger-med-delt-parkeringsareal.pdf`
**Category:** Charging infrastructure — guide (residential/multi-unit dwelling shared parking)
**Original language:** Danish (translated/summarized to English below; proper nouns kept in Danish)
**One-line summary:** A "mini-guide" by DTU Elektro (Technical University of Denmark, Dept. of Electrical Engineering), funded by Region Hovedstaden, giving Danish housing associations/companies ten recommendations across four stages for preparing shared parking areas at residential buildings for EV charging infrastructure.

## Context / Policy Framing
- References Denmark's **70% target for greenhouse gas emission reduction**, driving expected electrification of a large share of passenger transport within the next ten years.
- Anticipates improvements in EV selection, range, and price making EVs attractive to most Danes.
- Best/most convenient charging location = home, overnight.
- Target audience: residential buildings with shared parking, managed by housing associations/companies ("boligforeninger og selskaber"), covering owner-occupied ("ejer"), cooperative ("andel"), and rental ("leje") apartments (both private and non-profit/almennyttige).
- Core success criterion stated: reduce the time between a resident requesting a charging solution and that need being met on the building's parking area.

## 1. Investigation Stage

### 1.1 Operators and Consultants
- Buildings can use a consultant and/or contract with a charging operator ("ladeoperatør").
- An independent/impartial consultant can assist in planning and execution.
- Alternatively, contract a charging operator offering a complete tailored charging solution.
- Recommendation: survey the market and obtain quotes if engaging either.
- Even when using a consultant/operator, the guide's recommendations should still be applied.
- **Regulatory note:** a revision of "**Bekendtgørelse om sideaktiviteter i almene boligorganisationer m.v.**" (Executive Order on ancillary activities in non-profit housing organizations, retsinformation.dk/eli/lta/2015/451) now allows non-profit housing companies to establish EV charge points themselves.

### 1.2 Building Requirements
- At time of writing, the **"ladestanderbekendtgørelsen"** (Danish Charging Point Executive Order, retsinformation.dk/eli/lta/2020/181) does **not** require residential buildings to prepare charging infrastructure **unless** major renovation/reconstruction projects affecting the parking facility are undertaken — in which case **all affected parking spaces must be prepared for charging infrastructure**.
- **Trafik-, Bygge- og Boligstyrelsen** (Danish Transport, Construction and Housing Authority) was preparing guidance on the ladestanderbekendtgørelsen, expected **2nd half of 2020**.
- Guide's recommendation: regardless of the Executive Order's requirements, associations/companies should proactively plan and prepare for EV charging as early as possible; future revisions of the Executive Order may tighten requirements.

### 1.3 Your Circumstances and Needs
Key factors affecting feasibility:
- **Fixed vs. shared parking spaces** — shared spaces allow multiple residents to share charge outlets (potentially most cost-effective); fixed/assigned spaces require cabling to every individual space so each resident can have their own outlet.
- **Available power** — should investigate early how much power (amperes) is available in the building's switchboard(s) ("strømtavle(r)") and the distance to the parking area. Extra capacity can be purchased if needed.
  - **Connection fee ("tilslutningsbidrag"): 1,100 DKK (excl. VAT) per extra ampere**, from 25 amperes up to the nearest standard fuse size.
- **Cable routing** — investigate how cables can be routed from switchboard to parking spaces.
  - For paved/hard surface areas (asphalt, cobblestone, etc.), conduit trenching is more expensive: **price range 1,000–2,000 DKK per meter of installed empty conduit ("tomrør").**
  - For wall-mounted outlets (e.g., in a parking garage), cable trays/ducts ("kabelbakker/lister") can be used at lower cost.
- **Guest charging/identification** — recommend investigating support for visitor charging, requiring the chosen solution to support user identification and later billing.
- Above factors determine the necessary investment calculation.

### 1.4 Investment and Support/Subsidy Options
- Installing an AC charge outlet, incl. installation and connection fee, can cost **15,000–30,000 DKK**, heavily dependent on excavation costs and capacity upgrades.
- Free-standing charge points generally cost more than wall-mounted ones due to excavation work.
- Start-up costs mean **first outlets cost more per unit (up to 30,000 DKK)** than outlets added later (**down to 15,000 DKK**).
- No single solution fits all housing associations; each must explore its own installation/subsidy options.
- **"Processtrømsordningen"** (process-electricity tax rebate scheme): as of the document's writing, available **until end of 2020** ("frem til ultimo 2020"), offering a saving on electricity used for EV charging of approximately **0.88 DKK per kWh (88 øre/kWh)**. Requires a CVR-registered company (Danish central business register) to act as supplier to the charge points.

## 2. Planning Stage

### 2.1 Rollout Strategy
Rollout typically comprises **four phases**:
1. **Klargøring (Preparation)** — ensure switchboard access/capacity, ensure cables can be routed to parking spaces, install empty conduit or other provisions for later cabling.
2. **Sikring af tilstrækkelig strøm (Securing sufficient power)** — may require securing extra power for the parking area; can be a major cost due to possible excavation and connection fees. Existing capacity can be better utilized via load limiting (see 2.2).
3. **Kabellægning (Cable-laying to parking spaces)** — pulling cables from switchboard to parking space via the preparations made in phase 1.
4. **Installation af ladestandere (Charge point installation)** — chargers can be installed in phases as demand arises.

- Recommendation: Phase 1 (preparation) should be undertaken immediately by housing associations; phases 2–4 can follow as demand dictates.

### Technical Rollout Recommendations
- **Good earth/ground connection** — transition resistance to earth must be **under 50 ohm**. Some chargers/EVs require a good ground connection to function.
- **Don't skimp on cable thickness** — marginal cost of a thicker power cable (higher cross-section) is low relative to total installation cost, and future-proofs the infrastructure.
- **Charging coincidence/diversity factor ("Ladesamtidighed")** — typically, at most **30–40% of a parking area's EVs will charge simultaneously**, though this depends heavily on the number of cars and type of EV users. If in doubt, use load limiting (see 2.2).
- Key recommendation: the association should have a strategy that meets both early residents' charging needs and future needs when all residents' cars are electric.

### 2.2 Choosing the Right Charging Solution
- Recommend primarily **AC chargers** for normal charging (**11–22 kW**) using the European **Type 2 connector** (so-called "Mennekes" plug).
- Chargers can be free-standing or wall-mounted, with one or two outlets. A two-outlet charger can be placed between two parking spaces to charge two EVs simultaneously.
- **Sufficient charging power** — recommend a minimum feed of **11 kW (16 amps / 3-phase) per outlet** (22 kW to a two-outlet charger). This charging power gives approx. **1 km of driving range per minute of charging**. A short stay at the residence (1–2 hours) gives a reasonable range addition (**60–120 km**).
- **Use a proper charger, not a "granny cable"** — charging via existing standard sockets using a so-called "mormorkabel" (granny/grandmother cable) is discouraged. Per **Sikkerhedsstyrelsen** (Danish Safety Technology Authority), this can pose safety issues in some cases (depending on socket type and underlying installation), plus significantly lower charging speed and exclusion of smart functionality.
- **Smart chargers** — can connect to the internet for monitoring, control, and access management; enable smart charging when power is cheapest/greenest, load balancing for the local grid or parking-area capacity, and control of external (non-association) user access/billing. Chargers should support **OCPP 1.6 or 2.0** and implement cybersecurity — **only OCPP version 2.0 includes cybersecurity**, and it remains up to charger manufacturers to implement this part of the protocol. DTU and DEA (Dansk Elbil Alliance) planned to publish smart-charger recommendations later in 2020.
- **Load limiting ("Ladebegrænsning")** — if total EV load risks exceeding local capacity, consider chargers supporting **static or dynamic load limiting**, keeping combined power draw under a set ceiling.
  - **Static load limiting:** outlets get a fixed cap on combined consumption.
  - **Dynamic load limiting:** the cap changes continuously based on the building's other consumption, optimizing capacity utilization.
  - This should be considered during planning and typically requires all association members to use the same charger type.
- **Fast (DC) charging** — consider whether DC chargers are needed on a shared parking area.
  - **A 50 kW DC charger can cost 300,000–400,000 DKK** (incl. purchase and connection, excl. any grid connection fee).
  - **Grid connection fee for such a DC charger: 150,000 DKK.**
  - Can deliver **over 100 km of range in a half-hour charge**, useful for residents with urgent needs and visitors with short errands.
  - Recommend introducing **time-limited parking** when using this charger type.

### 2.3 Investment in Preparation
- Recommendation: it is undesirable for the necessary charging-infrastructure investment to fall solely on the first EV owners in an association.
- Recommend treating preparation ("klargøring") as a **collective investment** benefiting current and future residents, which can be combined with user fees spreading the cost as residents acquire EVs over time.
- Private housing companies can view this investment as future-proofing and a value increase to the company's property.
- **"Under jorden/over jorden" principle** ("underground/aboveground"): the association collectively handles the necessary electrical preparation, cabling, and capacity ("underground"), while the individual EV-owning resident or charging operator pays for the charger itself and its installation ("aboveground").

### 2.4 Resident Involvement
- Recommend involving residents early in decision-making/planning regardless of housing type; open dialogue on the necessity of investing in EV charging.
- At a general assembly ("generalforsamling"), discuss how the association will approach the first preparation and investment.
- If the housing association takes on a larger role in managing charging infrastructure, a **change to the association's bylaws ("vedtægtsændring")** may be necessary to clarify framework and responsibility.
- Recommend surveying how many members are considering EV purchase, to estimate how quickly demand for more charging spaces will grow.

## 3. Installation Stage

### 3.1 Choice of Installer
- All electrical work on charge boxes and their installation is **subject to authorization/licensing requirements** ("autorisationspligtigt").
- Advantageous to use an installer experienced specifically with EV charging infrastructure, since dimensioning EV installations differs from standard electrical installations.
- Even when preparing for 3-phase charging, many EVs still only charge on a single phase — recommend **"rotating" phases** between charge outlets to balance load as much as possible.
- Reiterates: ensure good ground connection — **transition resistance to earth must be under 50 ohm** for all EV types to charge.
- If recommended preparations were made, adding new charge points is relatively quick and simple.

## 4. Operations Stage

### 4.1 Responsibility for Operations
- Maintenance needs are generally relatively low.
- If a charging operator installed the association's chargers, this responsibility typically falls to that operator.
- Where the association itself procured the charging infrastructure, it's important to have a fault-handling procedure, e.g., via an agreement/contract with an electrical installer.
- Most important: ability to respond quickly to faults, ideally with a phone number/email for fault reporting displayed directly at the chargers.

## Relevance Notes for HDEV/Depot Tool (Claude's own framing, not from source)
- This document targets **light-duty/passenger EV residential charging**, not HD trucks or depots, but several concepts transfer directly to depot-scale planning: the four-phase rollout methodology (prepare → secure power → cable → install), the "under jorden/over jorden" (infrastructure vs. equipment) capex split, static/dynamic load limiting concepts, and the 30-40% simultaneous-charging diversity factor as an illustrative (light-duty-context) coincidence-factor reference point — useful as a comparison baseline against HD-fleet-specific coincidence factors that would need separate sourcing.
- The connection-fee/tomrør/cable cost figures are Danish grid-connection/civil-works cost references (residential-scale), useful only as an order-of-magnitude reference for low-power (11-22kW) infrastructure, not for HD DC charging economics.
