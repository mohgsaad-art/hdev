## Policy Context / Climate Targets

- Denmark's national climate targets cited: **reduce CO2 emissions by 70% by 2030**, and **reach net-zero emissions by 2050**.
- Van and truck transport in Denmark accounts for **10%** of Denmark's total CO2 emissions (national figure).
- In central parts of Danish cities specifically, municipal data shows van/truck traffic accounts for **up to 30-40%** of traffic-related CO2 emissions (a much higher local share than the 10% national average).

## The Problem / Challenge

Urban logistics today operates with no overall coordination — individual distributors drive into cities, unload (often disruptively to city life), and typically leave with empty vehicles.

**Negative consequences listed:**
- Overloading of road network and urban space, causing congestion
- Nuisance and health impacts from pollution and noise
- Accidents due to poor visibility
- Poor working environment
- High climate impact

To date, decarbonization efforts have focused almost exclusively on passenger transport (electric cars) and electrifying buses — van/truck freight has not been high on the agenda for municipal planners or politicians.

### Breakdown of CO2 contributors by logistics type (Netherlands data, cited by CONCITO)
The Dutch example is used as the best available breakdown since Danish data on emission sources by logistics type is described as poor ("ikke særlig gode tal"):

| Logistics type | Share of CO2 contribution (Netherlands) |
|---|---|

## Actors and Barriers (Table)

| Actor | Role/interest | Barrier |
|---|---|---|
| Varemodtagerne (goods recipients: shops, production companies, offices, restaurants, private individuals) | Want frequent deliveries but little interest/influence over how transport is organized | Requirements for zero-emission vehicles/cargo bikes and consolidating goods onto fewer vehicles could interfere with business models and raise costs; consumers accustomed to cheap/free delivery |
| Transportører og logistikansvarlige (carriers/logistics managers) | Focus on fast, cheap delivery; open to green transition if it doesn't distort competition | Logistics driven by tasks requiring personnel/equipment on-site that cannot be consolidated into a single green truck |
| Servicetransport (tradespeople, cleaning, etc.) — large share of van traffic | — | Conflict between customer demand for fast delivery and residents' desire for a calm local environment; few citizens make political demands for lower emissions from freight |
| Private individuals (customers, residents, citizens) | Mixed views | Same conflict as above |
| Kommunen (the municipality) | Must manage traffic flow while balancing environment and traffic safety | Can regulate traffic in various ways but struggles to intervene in the underlying logistics causing the problems |
| Staten (the state) | Sets overall framework for trade, transport, and municipal powers | Does not today take clear responsibility for regulating/developing urban logistics |

## Case Examples (Solutions)

### 1. Göteborg, Sweden — Stadsleveransen and Lindholmsleveransen
- Two systems: **Stadsleveransen** consolidates deliveries to shops in the pedestrian city-center zone; **Lindholmsleveransen** consolidates parcel deliveries to businesses in the Lindholmen urban development area.
- Mechanism: goods for the area are delivered to a terminal, repacked into small electric vehicles for last-mile delivery. Concept also covers return transport, packaging, and waste.
- **Results:** Stadsleveransen reduced heavy-vehicle traffic in the city center by **70%** measured in kilometers driven, and by **25%** measured in hours driven.
- No CO2 measurement was made, but since the initiative operates in only a small part of the city, the effect is described as modest in itself.
- The projects have received ongoing municipal financial support for many years, and it has proven difficult to make them financially self-sustaining.

### 2. Utrecht, Netherlands — Construction logistics hub
- ~10 years ago, the municipality decided, when establishing a new city district, to organize construction-material transport rationally to avoid congestion and environmental problems.
- The municipality established a hub where construction materials from various suppliers could be picked, packed, and distributed jointly via zero-emission vehicles to the building site. Vehicles carried construction waste back on the return trip. Worker transport to the area was also consolidated.
- The municipality developed its own software enabling suppliers to coordinate logistics, and succeeded in getting actors to deliver to the consolidation center without compulsion (voluntary participation).
- **Results:**
  - **70% reduction** in heavy transport trips to the construction site
  - **40% reduction** in personal (worker) trips
  - **70% reduction** in CO2 emissions — with a corresponding reduction in costs
  - A specific building project was completed in **28 weeks** versus an expected **41 weeks**
- The entire concept took nearly **10 years** to implement, mainly because it required breaking many habits/routines across companies.

### 3. Oslo, Norway — Municipal climate goals + hub partnerships
- Oslo municipality has set ambitious climate goals, including that **all vans/trucks entering the city center must run on sustainable energy by 2030**.
- The municipality committed that all of its own procurement will over time shift to zero-emission vans/trucks.
- To ease the transition, the municipality entered dialogue with three major carrier companies: **Schenker, PostNord, and Bring**.
- An area near the city center was designated where individual carriers could each establish "hubs" to transfer goods from heavy trucks to smaller zero-emission vehicles.
- The Norwegian state supported this with subsidies for purchasing electric vans, among other measures.
- **Results:** The three carriers expect **all deliveries in Oslo to be made with zero-emission vehicles within 3-5 years**. Several stakeholders felt that the positive "push and pull" from authorities accelerated the process more than rigid mandatory requirements would have.

## Three Policy Approaches Identified

1. **Demand-driven approach:** Service/goods recipients begin demanding low/zero-emission delivery. Works fastest where public bodies lead by example with green transport procurement (e.g., Oslo, Swedish municipal collaborations, early moves by Danish municipalities like Copenhagen). Limited by lack of a standard for "green delivery" and customer focus on cheap/fast delivery over green transport.
2. **Regulation-driven approach:** Public requirements on which transport may operate within parts of a city zone (e.g., zones reserved for zero-emission vehicles). Most extensive example: **the Netherlands**, where the state has legislated the rollout of **zero-emission zones in 30-40 cities from 2025 onward**. Challenge: translating requirements into practice without making deliveries unnecessarily expensive/difficult.
3. **Subsidy-driven approach:** State or municipalities support the transition via schemes such as support for shared goods consolidation centers (as in Göteborg), zero-emission vehicle acquisition (as in Norway), or financing both consolidation and software development (as in Utrecht). Challenge: costs tax revenue, and many models collapse once public subsidy is withdrawn. Subsidies should be simple and uniform to ensure equal competitive conditions.

## CONCITO's Recommendations

CONCITO recommends dialogue with all actors in a partnership, combining elements from the different approaches:

**At the state level:**
- A funding pool ("pulje") to support purchase of zero-emission vans/trucks and establishment of charging infrastructure, primarily at businesses.
- Legislation enabling municipalities to create locally-tailored solutions.
- Clear, long-term framework conditions for municipalities and businesses.

**At the municipal level:** Establish partnerships for climate-neutral city logistics (e.g., as part of **DK2020 klimaplaner** — DK2020 climate plans, a Danish municipal climate planning framework). Central elements could include:
- Promoting demand for green delivery, both in municipal procurement and through developing concrete standards/goals for green transport purchasing that can spread to larger buyers
- City municipalities should use the legal option for **pilot zero-emission zones** ("forsøg med nulemissionszoner")
- Partnerships should consider prioritizing parking for electric vehicles, establishing loading zones in relevant locations, designating specific truck routes, and establishing goods/parcel consolidation

**Three headline recommendations (as presented in the source's closing graphic):**
1. Fremme efterspørgslen efter grøn levering (Promote demand for green delivery)
2. Forsøg med nulemissionszoner (Pilot zero-emission zones)
3. Partnerskaber for klimaneutral citylogistik (Partnerships for climate-neutral city logistics)
