## 1. Regulatory and legislative status check

### 1.1 Energinet's prioritization model — live, but under a cloud

- Your knowledge base holds `ny-prioriteringsmodel-energinet-elnet-2026.md`, dated to a "1 February 2026" pamphlet — Energinet's own description of the new queue-prioritization model for **transmission-grid** connections, replacing first-come-first-served with a maturity-gate + prioritization-category system.
- **What's new:** Forsyningstilsynet (the energy regulator) issued a **påbud** (formal order) against Energinet, reported 7 September 2026, on the grounds that this model was never submitted to Forsyningstilsynet for approval before Energinet implemented it. [Source: transportmagasinet.dk, "Energinet får påbud for metode til at prioritere nye tilslutninger", 7 Sep 2026 — I could not extract whether Energinet has since paused, amended, or continued operating the model; a companion headline ("Energinet fortsætter sagsbehandling trods påbud") suggests Energinet kept processing cases regardless, but I did not verify this directly — flag as unconfirmed, worth a dedicated check before you rely on it.]
- **Why this matters for you:** your commercial narrative leans on "grid-friendliness is now a queue-position criterion" as a validated, citable fact (see `overview.md`). It still *directionally* is — but the specific administrative model is contested, and the real legal basis is catching up to it in primary legislation (next section). Pitching "Energinet's prioritization model requires X" as settled fact right now is riskier than it was a week ago. Pitching "the direction of travel — in law, not just administrative practice — is toward netvenlighed-based prioritization" is on firmer ground.

### 1.2 The actual legal basis is arriving separately: Bill L 23

- `20252-l23-som-fremsat-01.md` — a real bill (Lovforslag L 23, Folketing 2025-26, 2nd session), introduced 20 August 2026 by the Minister for Climate, Energy and Utilities, proposed entry into force **12 October 2026**.
- This is the bill that gives Energinet/grid companies actual statutory authority to prioritize connection requests by category and "netvenlighed" (grid-friendliness), and to refuse Category 3/4 requests. It creates **four priority categories**: (1) protected/ordinary household & critical-function demand, (2) general business/electrification projects (this is where most fleet depots and HDEV charging would likely sit), (3) energy storage (batteries), (4) large power consumers (data centers).
- Notably: **"certain publicly accessible EV charging-infrastructure projects for road vehicles may also fall in Category 1"** — i.e., some charging infrastructure gets the *most* protected priority tier, not the least. This is a genuinely new, specific, and citable data point for the HDEV product story: depot/fleet charging infrastructure's queue position depends heavily on whether it can be framed as Category 1 (protected) vs. Category 2 (general). That's a live classification question worth building into your tool's assumptions or at least your sales narrative.
- The bill is explicitly the *legal foundation* the February 2026 Energinet prioritization model needed and didn't have — so the påbud in §1.1 and this bill are directly connected: Energinet moved ahead of its own legal authority, and the Ministry is now retroactively building that authority via L23, entry into force 12 October 2026.
- **Practical read:** the regulatory story here isn't "settled" or "broken" — it's "in the middle of being legislated, right now, this autumn." That's actually a *better* pitch than a static fact: "the rules determining your queue position are being written into law as of October 2026, and grid-friendliness is one of the four determining categories" is more urgent and more current than citing a pamphlet from February.

## 4. What's working for other players, what's not, and why

**Working, and why (patterns across the case-study compendium and vendor material):**
- **Fixed-route, fixed-schedule municipal fleets (home care, waste collection, road maintenance) electrify cleanly and early** — this shows up over and over (Dragør, Ballerup, Ishøj, Frederiksberg elskraldebil, Køge) because range anxiety and charging-window uncertainty — the two hardest variables — are close to zero. This is the segment where "rule of thumb" sizing genuinely is good enough, which is also why nobody's built a sophisticated tool for it. It's a *validation* market for HDEV (cheap to model, easy to win), not the *value* market.
- **Vendor-agnostic advisory positioning wins trust** — City Tekniq, Elento, and the whole "uvildig rådgivning" (impartial advice) framing across the Region Hovedstaden guides all lean on the same insight: businesses trust an advisor more when the advisor doesn't also sell them a charger. This validates HDEV's own "vendor-neutral, VECTO-auditable" positioning in your memory — it's not a novel insight, it's table stakes in this market, which means you need to be *credibly* independent (auditable methodology, cited sources — which you already insist on), not just claim to be.
- **Battery-buffered/behind-the-meter charging (Nerve Smart Systems) has a genuinely strong, cited ROI case** (12–18 months, grid-connection cost savings up to 500,000 DKK) for sites where grid capacity is the binding constraint — directly complementary to your DK grid-connection-delay thesis. Worth knowing as a hardware-layer solution you could model *around*, not compete with.

**Not working, and why:**
- **Municipal urban-logistics consolidation hubs (Concito case studies: Göteborg, Utrecht, Oslo) struggle to self-fund** — real, large CO2/traffic reductions (up to 70%) but "ongoing municipal financial support for many years" and "difficult to make financially self-sustaining." Pattern: strong externality case, weak private unit economics. Relevant if you ever get pulled toward "logistics hub sizing" as an adjacent product — the case studies say don't, unless you can attach it to a paying private off-taker.
- **The charge-operator market itself is described, verbatim, by a business owner in your own knowledge base, as "extremely hard to navigate"** (`virksomhedsguide-2023-ladeinfrastruktur.md`, DME case) — most operators can't be called, only messaged via chatbot, and the hardware market, while easier to browse, is hard to evaluate for future-proofing. This is a second, distinct gap from the sizing gap: an independent, comparative "which operator/hardware fits your situation" layer. Not your core wedge, but a very plausible upsell or dashboard feature once you have the client relationship (which is exactly the HC/Elento model's actual value-add today, done manually).
- **Generalized/one-size-fits-all flexibility tools "collapsed" as a business idea per your own PART 2 summary notes** — the reasoning there (flexibility window can't be known without site visit or telemetry for arbitrary assets) is the same underlying logic that makes generic TCO calculators (the COWI Excel tool, elbilviden.dk) weaker than a physics-grounded, vehicle-and-route-specific tool: generic inputs produce generic, unauditable outputs. This is a second independent confirmation of your existing BESS-vs-generalized-flexibility conclusion, from a completely different part of your research.

**Business-model implication:** the market pattern across every "working" example is *advisory relationship first, tooling second* — nobody is buying a sizing tool cold; they're buying a relationship with someone who de-risks a large capex decision, and the tool is how that person is more right than a competitor. This is consistent with, and reinforces, your existing modular-pricing-through-a-relationship model with HC rather than a self-serve SaaS-first approach — at least until you have named reference clients.

## 7. LCA / TCO / carbon-footprint tools — do they clash with VECTO?

**Short answer: no clash — they're complementary layers, and VECTO sits underneath them, not beside them.**

- VECTO is a **physics simulation engine**: given a vehicle's mechanical/aerodynamic parameters and a driving cycle, it calculates energy/fuel consumption and CO2 output. It has no concept of DKK cost, financing, tax regime, or cradle-to-grave production emissions.
- The Region Hovedstaden/COWI **TCO tool** and **LCA tool** (§6, and the Excel file you already have) are **downstream cost/emissions accounting layers**: they take a consumption figure (kWh/100km or l/100km) as a *given input* and turn it into DKK totals (TCO) or full lifecycle CO2e totals (LCA, including battery/body production emissions that VECTO doesn't touch at all). They are not trying to calculate consumption from physics — they're trying to compare drivetrains once you already know (or guess) the consumption number.
- **This means your engine's natural role is to be the missing input layer for tools like this** — VECTO-grounded consumption physics feeding a COWI-style TCO/LCA output layer — rather than a competing methodology. There's no methodological conflict to resolve; the honest gap is that the existing public tools' consumption numbers are user-typed guesses, and yours would be calculated. If you ever wanted to make this claim publicly or to a client, the COWI Excel tool is a clean, citable, concrete example to point to (their own methodology document even cites ICCT, T&E, and IVL for the *other* lifecycle stages — they clearly did serious work on the LCA side; the consumption-input side is just outside their scope, not a mistake on their part).
- One nuance worth flagging as your own synthesis, not a sourced fact: LCA and TCO tools generally need only *one* consumption number per vehicle-scenario (average kWh/100km), where your engine produces a full time-series (P_wheel, SOC trace, etc.). If you ever build a "feed HDEV's output into a TCO/LCA calculator" feature, the simplest version is just exposing the time-averaged consumption figure — the richer time-series data is for your own charging/depot-sizing layer, not something existing TCO/LCA tools are built to consume.

## 10. Timeline / pipeline per segment, and where you fit

Based on the guides and case studies (dates as stated in source docs):

- **Municipalities** — furthest along, oldest adopters (home-care EVs since ~2014–2015 per the Fagfolk compendium), now in a **compliance-driven phase**: statutory charger-installation deadlines (2025, per the ladestanderbekendtgørelse) have already passed or are imminent, and the live open question is fleet electrification depth (light EVs are largely done; heavier municipal vehicles — waste collection, road/park equipment — are the current frontier, per `renovationskoersel-pa-el-2023.md` and `elektriske-non-road-maskiner-2023.md`). **This is where HDEV's HD-vehicle physics modelling has the clearest immediate pull**, because the easy (light-vehicle) part of municipal electrification is essentially solved by existing rule-of-thumb tools, and the hard part (RCVs, road-maintenance vehicles, non-road machinery) is exactly where nobody has a proper sizing tool.
- **Housing associations** — mid-pipeline, now entering a **rights-driven acceleration phase**: the 1 January 2026 statutory right to install without general-assembly approval (per your memory, flagged in §1.3 for re-verification) removes the main historical blocker (governance friction), which should sharply increase near-term demand for exactly the kind of "how much capacity do I actually need at my switchboard" advisory HC and City Tekniq already do well. This segment's technical sizing problem is genuinely simpler than HD fleets (passenger EVs, predictable overnight charging, existing well-validated rule-of-thumb tables) — so HDEV's specific physics-engine differentiator matters less here; the value-add is more procedural/governance/coincidence-factor modelling than route-physics modelling.
- **Fleet/haulage owners** — earliest-stage relative to potential, still largely in a **first-mover/pilot phase** per the case studies (DFDS, Unicon, Dansk Cater cited as recent, named pilots) — driven by the new kilometer-based road charge (making diesel relatively more expensive) and public-sector green-procurement pressure on their customers. **This is the segment where the regulatory story in §1 (grid-connection queue, prioritization categories) bites hardest**, because a haulage depot's grid connection is large enough to actually hit Energinet's category system, unlike a single housing-association switchboard upgrade. This is also where the ITD/heavy-transport thread from the HC partnership would land, if it converts from aspirational to live.

**Where HDEV fits across all three:** the common thread is that every segment's guide treats "how much energy will this actually need" as an open, deferred, or rule-of-thumb question — and the *cost* of getting that number wrong scales with vehicle size and depot investment. That cost is lowest for municipalities' existing light-EV fleets (already solved, low stakes), moderate for housing associations (governance-gated, technically simple), and highest for HD fleet/haulage depots (technically hard, high capex, and now directly gated by the Energinet Category 1–4 queue system in §1.2). That ordering — housing associations easiest-to-sell-fastest due to the new legal right, but fleet/haulage highest-value-per-engagement due to complexity and capex — is a genuine strategic tension worth being explicit about with HC, since his strongest documented credibility (housing associations) and his highest ambition (ITD/heavy-transport) point in different directions on this axis.

## Addendum (20 Sep 2026, follow-up digging) — Ladepunktsberegner detail, and correcting the Elindsigt2.2 framing

### A. The Ladepunktsberegner (Charging Point Calculator) — what it actually is

- **Origin:** built under **FUSE (Frederiksberg Urban Smart Electromobility)**, a project run by **Dansk e-Mobilitet**. Developers credited: **DTU, Hybrid Greentech, Dansk e-Mobilitet, and COWI**. The calculation method itself is attributed to **Hybrid Greentech**. [Sources: danskemobilitet.dk/fuse-frederiksberg-urban-smart-electromobility/charge-point-calculator; elbilviden.dk/offentlig/ladeinfrastruktur/ladepunktsberegner-til-kommuner/]
- **What it estimates:** public charging-point need for **private citizens only** — the tool's own scope note explicitly **excludes commercial driving and tourism-related charging need**. That's an important boundary for you: it is not a fleet/depot tool at all, by its own design, and its output would not be a valid proxy for HD fleet/depot sizing even at the municipal level.
- **Granularity:** calculated at **town level**, with a municipality's total being the sum of its towns' individual estimates — i.e., it's disaggregated geographically within a municipality, not a single top-down municipal number.
- **Data sources cited:** transport habit surveys, Danmarks Statistik (Statistics Denmark), a named Danish charging operator's data, and the Danish Energy Agency (Energistyrelsen).
- **Versioning:** this is an evolution of an original 2019 DTU report/methodology — current version has updated daily-charging-consumption-per-charge-point figures and now explicitly accounts for "the potential in housing associations and workplaces" as a factor reducing residual *public* charging need (i.e., it nets out private/workplace charging capacity before estimating the public gap).
- **Stated conservatism:** the tool's own documentation describes its results as **"a conservative (minimum) estimate"** of public charging infrastructure need — worth remembering if you ever cite its output as a ceiling rather than a floor.
- **Format:** downloadable as an Excel file (named on the elbilviden.dk page as `ChargePointCalculator_v5-4.xlsx`), not a web-interactive calculator — same distribution pattern as the COWI TCO/LCA tool already in your project files.
- **UPDATE (you supplied the actual report and the actual `ChargePointCalculator_v5.xlsx` — full methodology now verified, both from the report text and by reading the live Excel formulas directly):**

**Source document:** "Ladepunktsberegneren — til danske byer og kommuner," Final, 29 Nov 2022. Authors/funders: Hybrid Greentech (method), Dansk e-Mobilitet, COWI, DTU. Financed by EUDP through the FUSE project (fuse-project.dk). Data: Transportvaneundersøgelsen (TU), Danmarks Statistik, "en stor dansk lade-operatør" (a large Danish charging operator, unnamed), Energistyrelsen. Predecessor: "Sådan skaber Danmark grøn infrastruktur til én million elbiler" (DTU + Dansk Elbil Alliance, 2019) — this tool explicitly supersedes and refines that report's estimates.

**The three headline outputs, and exactly how they're computed (confirmed against both the report text and the live formulas in `ChargePointCalculator_v5.xlsx`, sheet "Ladere," per-town rows):**

- **S** = total daily public-transport-electricity demand for a town (kWh/day) = `(1 + 14%) × EVs_in_town × avg_km_per_car_per_day / 5`. The `/5` is the fixed consumption factor: the tool uses a flat **200 Wh/km** (0.2 kWh/km) for every vehicle, so dividing km by 5 converts it straight to kWh — this is explicitly flagged in the report as a simplification, chosen to represent winter-half-year average consumption for a Nissan Leaf including charging losses, **not per-vehicle-model-specific**. The **+14%** is a Friday/pre-holiday-weekday adjustment: Danish cars drive 14% farther on Fridays than the year-round average (49 km vs. 51 km/day per Table 1 in the report, sourced to the national travel survey), and the tool deliberately dimensions to that near-peak day rather than the average day.
- **D1** (offentlige + semioffentlige ladepunkter behov) = `ROUND( S × (1 − A) / 32, 0)` — i.e., the share of daily demand NOT covered by home charging (`1 − A`, where A = the town's share of car-owning residents who can charge on their own property, sourced from Transportvaneundersøgelsen parking-condition data), converted into a **count of charge points** by dividing by **32 kWh/day** — the empirical average daily throughput of a real public "destinationslader" in the charging operator's network (from their actual histogram of per-charger daily kWh delivered, Figure 15 in the report).
- **D2** (minimum purely-public charge points) = `D1 − [S×B/32] − [S×(1−A−B)×C/32]` — i.e., D1 minus the charge points that could instead be met by housing-association charging (B = share with shared-parking charging access) and by workplace charging (C = share with workplace charging access, applied only to the residual population not already covered by A or B).
- **E** (lynladere/fast chargers for everyday, non-long-trip charging) = `15% × D1 × 32 / 215` — 15% of D1's underlying kWh demand is assumed to be served by fast chargers instead of normal ones (an empirical split from the same operator's real usage data), and **215 kWh/day** is the empirical average daily throughput of a real public fast-charger outlet in that network (their Figure 16 histogram; a 2-outlet fast-charging station therefore averages ~430 kWh/day).
- **Separately, long-distance/range-extension fast chargers** are computed from the *national* distribution of daily driving distance (Figure 3 in the report: 86% of car-trips are under 100 km, 98.6% under 300 km) against an assumed 300 km average EV range, 100% SOC at departure, and a 20% SOC floor on arrival — i.e., only the ~1.4% of trips over 300 km (but 13% of total km driven) need a top-up, and the excess-km-beyond-buffer for that population is converted to kWh at the same 0.2 kWh/km and divided by 215 kWh/day/charger. Verified directly in the `Extension` sheet's formulas.
- A, B, C are **not single national constants** — they're computed per-town from Transportvaneundersøgelsen, and towns with too few survey interviews (N≤500) are pooled with other towns of similar size to stay statistically significant (Figure 10/14 in the report). National averages: A=78%, B=13%, C=55%. Two extremes cited in the report: Frederiksberg (dense, apartment-heavy) has A=16%/B=24%/C=39%; Herning (dispersed, single-family-house-heavy) has A=86%/B=10%/C=58% — a nearly 5x difference in A alone, which is why D1 for Frederiksberg (2,133 charge points by 2030) outstrips Herning (742) despite Herning having more cars.
- EV fleet growth per municipality is a **Bass diffusion model**, calibrated to each municipality's actual EV share as of spring 2022, all converging toward a national trajectory of 1 million BEVs by 2030 and 3.8 million (100%) by 2050 — confirmed in the `Reference` sheet, which holds year-by-year projected EV counts per kommune from 2022 onward.
- **National headline results** (Table/Figure 17): 2022 — D1=5,720, D2=2,135, EVs=79,657. **2030 — D1=67,046, D2=23,664, EVs≈978,240.** Fast chargers nationally: 324 total in 2022 (217 range-extension + 107 everyday-in-cities) rising to 4,327 in 2030 (2,853 + 1,474) — the report notes the *current pace of commercial fast-charger rollout* already looks likely to outstrip the 2030 figure by 2024, based on announced industry installations.

**The decisive finding for your coincidence-factor question: this tool has no coincidence factor, no simultaneity factor, and no peak-power/grid-capacity concept anywhere in it — confirmed by reading every formula in the workbook, not just the report text.** It sizes a **count of charge points**, not kW or amperes, and the entire "how hard is each charge point actually used" question is collapsed into two single empirical constants (32 kWh/day/normal point, 215 kWh/day/fast point) pulled from one operator's real network averages — an *average utilization* assumption, not a *peak/coincidence* one. It converts to charger COUNT, never to a grid-capacity or ampere figure at any point in the chain. This is a genuinely different tool class from a coincidence-factor/headroom calculation — it answers "how many charge points does this town need," not "how much power will they draw at once" or "does the grid connection have room." That's a clean, useful distinction to have confirmed with certainty rather than inferred from a marketing page.
- **Related, higher-fidelity sibling tool — the GAIA model:** also under FUSE, described as "a cross-field collaboration between DTU Management and DTU Wind and Energy Systems," used specifically to investigate charging-infrastructure need **and the resulting flexibility and distribution-grid impact** at Frederiksberg. Unlike the Ladepunktsberegner, this sounds architecturally closer to the grid-capacity/coincidence question — but I only reached a promotional page with no formulas or parameters disclosed; the underlying citation is likely Andreas Thingvad's DTU PhD thesis work (his 2021 thesis and the 2021 eTransportation paper with Peter Bach Andersen et al. are both cited as references [1] and [3] in the Ladepunktsberegner report itself) — worth a direct search for that thesis/paper if the grid-impact angle matters to you.

**Bottom line on this tool:** it's a real, rigorously documented, multi-institution methodology (full citation trail: Thingvad et al. 2021 eTransportation; Jakobsen/Flader/Andersen/Thingvad/Bollerslev 2019 DEA/DTU report; Thingvad PhD thesis 2021, DTU), scoped explicitly and only to *passenger-EV public charging* (commercial/fleet/tourism traffic excluded by the authors' own stated scope), and it never touches power/capacity/coincidence at all — only charge-point counts from average daily throughput. This sharpens, rather than undermines, the point in §3 of the main report: even DTU's own purpose-built municipal tool, built with real network data and serious academic rigor, stops at "how many sockets," and never asks "how many kW at once" — which is exactly the physics-and-coincidence layer your engine is built to answer, for a segment (HD fleet depots) this tool explicitly disclaims from the outset.

### B. Elindsigt2.2.xlsx — correcting my earlier framing on coincidence/load factor

You asked me to think of this tool specifically as a way to find the actual coincidence factor or load factor for a capacity-headroom question. Having dug into its actual cells and formulas rather than just its structure: **it doesn't compute that — but it does assemble exactly the two raw ingredients you'd need to compute it yourself, and it shows a working pattern for pulling both from an official Danish data source.**

What it actually has:
- **A real hourly consumption trace** — the "Timespecifikation" sheet holds per-hour kWh consumption (alongside per-hour cost-component breakdowns: supplier subscription, net/DSO tariff, TSO system tariff, TSO transmission tariff, surcharges), pulled live via the **eloverblik.dk** API using a personal access token. This is a genuine per-meter load profile, not an aggregate/statistical one.
- **The connection's actual contracted capacity** — labelled **"Effektgrænse Ampere"** (power/capacity limit, in amperes) = "Leveringsomfang" (delivery scope). This is pulled automatically from eloverblik.dk for that specific metering point, with a manual-entry fallback if eloverblik.dk doesn't expose it for that meter type.
- **A DSO peak-tariff time window as a billing dimension** — the workbook's data model tags consumption by a "Tariflast" category including **"Spidslast"** (peak load), which off the "Din Regning" sheet appears to be a fixed clock-time band (e.g. 17:00–21:00) used by the grid company for time-differentiated tariff billing. **This is the DSO's own definition of "peak hours" for pricing purposes — it is not a computed measure of how hard this specific meter is actually loaded relative to its own capacity.** I want to be precise about that distinction, because it's easy to conflate "peak tariff period" with "load/coincidence factor" and they're not the same thing.

What it does **not** contain, as far as I could find by scanning every sheet's cells and formulas for the relevant terms: no formula computing peak-demand-vs-effektgrænse headroom, and no formula computing a load factor (average load ÷ peak load) or a coincidence/diversity factor across multiple meters. It's a **bill-transparency tool**, not a capacity-planning tool — its job is "what am I actually paying for and why," not "how much spare capacity do I have."

**Why this is still directly useful to you, reframed correctly:** it's a working, tested example — built by someone with real DSO-side data expertise (the credited author lists himself as "Dataspecialist @ TREFOR El-net Øst," a Danish grid company) — of pulling both a real hourly load trace **and** the real contracted ampere limit for a specific metering point from **eloverblik.dk**, Denmark's official consumer/business energy-data platform, using nothing but a token the meter's owner controls. Danmarks Statistik and Energinet aside, this confirms eloverblik.dk exposes exactly the two numbers your capacity-headroom question needs (actual historical peak/average draw, and contracted capacity limit) at the individual metering-point level — for a household in this tool's current form, but the same API mechanism would apply to any commercial or depot metering point the tool's owner has access to. The coincidence-factor/headroom calculation itself — peak kW (from the hourly kWh trace) vs. effektgrænse converted to kW, and average/peak for load factor — is a small, well-defined layer you'd be adding on top of data this pattern already proves is pullable. That's a meaningfully different (and better) starting point than the 30–40% generic coincidence-factor assumption used throughout the passenger-EV housing-association guides in the main report (§9), because it would be measured from a specific site's actual history rather than assumed from a rule of thumb.

One caveat worth flagging: eloverblik.dk access requires the metering point owner's own MitID login and token — so this pattern only works where you (or a client, with their consent) can actually authenticate as the account/meter holder. It's not a way to pull a third party's consumption data without their cooperation.
